Section 01: Foundational Physics

Mathematical Prerequisites

Every later section leans on seven pieces of machinery: separation of variables in a central potential, the exact hydrogen solution, static and dynamic perturbation theory, angular-momentum coupling, the density-matrix treatment of a driven, decaying two-level system, and the units/constants conventions that tie atomic and SI quantities together. Rather than re-deriving pieces of these ad hoc wherever they are needed, this section builds all seven from first principles, once, rigorously. Later sections link back here instead of restating results.

A. Central Potentials: Separation of Variables

Every atomic structure calculation in this pipeline (the exact hydrogen solution, the quantum-defect model, the 3D orbital renderer) starts from the same starting point: a single particle of mass $m$ moving in a potential $V(r)$ that depends only on distance from a fixed center. The time-independent Schrödinger equation is

3D time-independent Schrödinger equation
$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf r) + V(r)\psi(\mathbf r) = E\psi(\mathbf r)$$

In spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\phi)$ the Laplacian is not obvious from Cartesian $\nabla^2=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2+\partial_z^2$. Changing variables with $x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z=r\cos\theta$ and applying the chain rule (or equivalently writing $\nabla^2$ in an orthogonal curvilinear system with scale factors $h_r=1$, $h_\theta=r$, $h_\phi=r\sin\theta$) gives

Laplacian in spherical coordinates
$$\nabla^2 =\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right) +\frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right) +\frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}$$

Group the angular derivatives into one operator:

$$\nabla^2 =\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right) +\frac{1}{r^2}\underbrace{\left[ \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right) +\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2} \right]}_{\equiv\ -\hat L^2/\hbar^2}$$

The bracketed operator is (up to the sign and $\hbar^2$) exactly the quantum-mechanical orbital angular momentum operator $\hat L^2$. Starting from the Cartesian definition $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ with $\mathbf p=-i\hbar\nabla$, the components are $\hat L_x=-i\hbar(y\partial_z-z\partial_y)$ and cyclic permutations. Converting to spherical coordinates with the same $(x,y,z)\leftrightarrow(r,\theta,\phi)$ map and the chain rule gives $\hat L_z=-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial\phi$. Assembling $\hat L^2=\hat L_x^2+\hat L_y^2+\hat L_z^2$ and simplifying the cross terms yields

$$\hat L^2 = -\hbar^2\left[\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}\right],\qquad \hat L_z=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$$

Because $V=V(r)$ contains no angular dependence, $[\hat L^2,H]=[\hat L_z,H]=0$, so we may seek simultaneous eigenstates of $H$, $\hat L^2$, and $\hat L_z$.

Separation of variables: explicit steps

Step 1. Write the product ansatz $\psi(r,\theta,\phi)=R(r)\,Y(\theta,\phi)$. Then

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}\right) =Y\,\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right),\qquad \hat L^2\psi = R\,\hat L^2 Y$$

so the Laplacian acting on the product is

$$\nabla^2\psi =Y\,\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) -\frac{R}{r^2}\frac{\hat L^2 Y}{\hbar^2}$$

Step 2. Insert into the Schrödinger equation $-(\hbar^2/2m)\nabla^2\psi+V(r)\psi=E\psi$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,Y\,\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) +\frac{1}{2m}\,R\,\frac{\hat L^2 Y}{r^2} +V(r)\,R\,Y =E\,R\,Y$$

Step 3. Multiply through by $2m r^2/(R Y)$ (assuming $R Y\neq0$ in the region of interest). Term by term:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) +\frac{1}{Y}\hat L^2 Y +2m r^2\bigl(V(r)-E\bigr) =0$$

Everything involving only $R(r)$ and everything involving only $Y(\theta,\phi)$ now sit separately:

Separation of variables (after multiplying by $2mr^2/(RY)$)
$$\underbrace{ -\frac{\hbar^2}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) +2m r^2\bigl(V(r)-E\bigr) }_{\text{function of }r\text{ only}} +\underbrace{\frac{1}{Y}\hat L^2 Y}_{\text{function of }\theta,\phi\text{ only}} =0$$

Equivalently, move the angular term to the other side:

Separated equations share one constant
$$\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) +\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}r^2\bigl(E-V(r)\bigr) =\frac{1}{Y}\frac{\hat L^2 Y}{\hbar^2} =\Lambda\quad\text{(separation constant)}$$

A function of $r$ alone can equal a function of $(\theta,\phi)$ alone only if both equal the same constant $\Lambda$.

Step 4 (angular): separate $\theta$ and $\phi$. The angular equation is $\hat L^2 Y=\hbar^2\Lambda\,Y$. Write out $\hat L^2$ and set $Y(\theta,\phi)=\Theta(\theta)\,\Phi(\phi)$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{\Theta\Phi}\left[ \frac{\Phi}{\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) +\frac{\Theta}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\phi^2} \right] =\hbar^2\Lambda$$

Multiply by $\sin^2\theta\,(\Theta\Phi)/(-\hbar^2)$ and rearrange so that every $\phi$-only term sits alone:

$$\frac{1}{\Phi}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\phi^2} =-\frac{\sin\theta}{\Theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) -\Lambda\sin^2\theta =-m^2\quad\text{(second separation constant)}$$

The left side depends only on $\phi$, the right only on $\theta$, so both equal a constant. We write that constant as $-m^2$ (the sign will make $\Phi$ oscillatory). Solve $\Phi''+m^2\Phi=0$: $\Phi(\phi)=e^{im\phi}$ (or $\cos m\phi$, $\sin m\phi$). Single-valuedness on the circle, $\Phi(\phi+2\pi)=\Phi(\phi)$, forces $m\in\mathbb{Z}$. Equivalently, $\hat L_z Y=\hbar m\,Y$ with $\hat L_z=-i\hbar\partial_\phi$.

The remaining $\theta$ equation is

$$\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) +\left[\Lambda-\frac{m^2}{\sin^2\theta}\right]\Theta=0$$

Change variable to $x=\cos\theta$ ($dx=-\sin\theta\,d\theta$). Then $\Theta(\theta)$ becomes a function $P(x)$ on $x\in[-1,1]$, and the ODE becomes the associated Legendre equation

$$\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[(1-x^2)\frac{dP}{dx}\right] +\left[\Lambda-\frac{m^2}{1-x^2}\right]P=0$$

Truncation of the Legendre series (why $\Lambda=l(l+1)$)

Treat first the axisymmetric case $m=0$ (ordinary Legendre equation). Expanding the derivative form gives

$$(1-x^2)P''-2x\,P'+\Lambda\,P=0$$

Seek a power series about the ordinary point $x=0$:

$$P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k x^k,\qquad P'(x)=\sum_{k=1}^\infty k a_k x^{k-1},\qquad P''(x)=\sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1)a_k x^{k-2}$$

Insert into $(1-x^2)P''-2x\,P'+\Lambda\,P=0$ and expand each piece as a series in powers of $x$:

$$P''=\sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1)a_k x^{k-2} =\sum_{j=0}^\infty (j+2)(j+1)a_{j+2}\,x^j \quad(j=k-2)$$ $$-x^2 P''=-\sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1)a_k x^{k}$$ $$-2x\,P'=-2\sum_{k=1}^\infty k a_k x^{k}$$ $$\Lambda\,P=\Lambda\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k x^{k}$$

Rename the dummy index $j\to k$ in the $P''$ line so every sum runs over the same power $x^k$. Collecting the coefficient of $x^k$ (for each $k=0,1,2,\ldots$) and setting it to zero gives

$$(k+2)(k+1)a_{k+2} -\bigl[k(k-1)+2k-\Lambda\bigr]a_k =0$$

Simplify the bracket: $k(k-1)+2k=k^2-k+2k=k(k+1)$. Solving for $a_{k+2}$ yields the two-step recursion

$$a_{k+2}=\frac{k(k+1)-\Lambda}{(k+1)(k+2)}\,a_k\qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots)$$

Why even and odd powers decouple. The recursion jumps by $2$ in the index: $a_0$ determines $a_2$, then $a_4$, then $a_6$, and so on; $a_1$ determines $a_3$, then $a_5$, then $a_7$, and so on. Nothing in the formula ever mixes an even-indexed coefficient with an odd-indexed one. So the general solution splits into two independent series of the same functional form:

$$P_{\mathrm{even}}(x)=a_0+a_2 x^2+a_4 x^4+\cdots,\qquad P_{\mathrm{odd}}(x)=a_1 x+a_3 x^3+a_5 x^5+\cdots$$

with $a_0$ and $a_1$ as the two free constants expected for a second-order ODE. Both chains use exactly the same ratio $a_{k+2}/a_k$.

For large $k$, $a_{k+2}/a_k\to 1$, so an infinite series behaves near $|x|\to1$ like a geometric series in $x^2$: it diverges at the endpoints $x=\pm1$ (the north and south poles $\theta=0,\pi$). A wavefunction that diverges at the poles is not acceptable on the sphere.

The only way both series stay finite at $x=\pm1$ is for one of them to terminate as a polynomial and the other to be set to zero. Termination at degree $l$ means the numerator vanishes when $k=l$:

Legendre truncation condition
$$l(l+1)-\Lambda=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\Lambda=l(l+1),\quad l=0,1,2,\ldots$$

Then $a_{l+2}=a_{l+4}=\cdots=0$, so $P$ is a polynomial of exact degree $l$. Choosing $a_1=0$ (even $l$) or $a_0=0$ (odd $l$) kills the non-terminating parity sector. The resulting polynomials, normalized by $P_l(1)=1$, are the Legendre polynomials $P_l(x)$. Explicitly from the recursion with that normalization:

$$P_0(x)=1,\qquad P_1(x)=x,\qquad P_2(x)=\tfrac12(3x^2-1),\qquad P_3(x)=\tfrac12(5x^3-3x)$$ $$P_4(x)=\tfrac18(35x^4-30x^2+3),\qquad P_5(x)=\tfrac18(63x^5-70x^3+15x)$$

Rodrigues' formula packages the same family without series bookkeeping:

$$P_l(x)=\frac{1}{2^l l!}\frac{d^l}{dx^l}(x^2-1)^l$$

and implies the Bonnet recurrence used in numerical codes, $(l+1)P_{l+1}=(2l+1)x\,P_l-l\,P_{l-1}$, together with the orthogonality relation on $[-1,1]$:

$$\int_{-1}^{1}P_{l'}(x)P_l(x)\,dx=\frac{2}{2l+1}\,\delta_{ll'}$$

(change of variables $x=\cos\theta$ turns this into the $\theta$-integral that normalizes $Y_l^0$). For $m\neq0$, strip the singular $1/(1-x^2)$ factor by writing $P(x)=(1-x^2)^{|m|/2}Q(x)$. Let $\mu=|m|$. Substituting into the associated Legendre equation and clearing the common prefactor $(1-x^2)^{\mu/2}$ produces an equation for $Q$ alone:

$$(1-x^2)Q''-2(\mu+1)x\,Q' +\bigl[\Lambda-\mu(\mu+1)\bigr]Q=0$$

This is again a Fuchsian ODE with an ordinary point at $x=0$. A power series $Q=\sum b_k x^k$ yields a two-step recursion of the same type as for $P_l$, and finiteness at $x=\pm1$ forces truncation with $\Lambda=l(l+1)$ and polynomial degree $l-\mu\ge0$. Hence

$$l=|m|,\,|m|+1,\,|m|+2,\,\ldots \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad l=0,1,2,\ldots\text{ and }|m|\le l$$

The regular solutions are the associated Legendre functions, obtained from $P_l$ by differentiating $|m|$ times and restoring the prefactor (Condon-Shortley phase $(-1)^m$ for $m\ge0$):

$$P_l^m(x)=(-1)^m(1-x^2)^{m/2}\frac{d^m}{dx^m}P_l(x),\qquad m=0,1,\ldots,l$$

For negative $m$, the standard convention is $P_l^{-m}(x)=(-1)^m\dfrac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}P_l^m(x)$, which keeps $Y_l^{-m}=(-1)^m(Y_l^m)^*$ consistent below. If $|m|>l$, the $m$-th derivative of a degree-$l$ polynomial vanishes, so $P_l^m\equiv0$: there is simply no regular solution. That is the precise origin of $|m|\le l$.

$$\hat L^2 Y=\hbar^2 l(l+1)\,Y,\qquad \Lambda=l(l+1)$$

So $\hbar^2 l(l+1)$ is not an arbitrary label: it is the discrete spectrum forced by demanding that $Y$ be finite and single-valued everywhere on the sphere.

Spherical harmonics from $\Theta\Phi$

Assemble $Y(\theta,\phi)=\Theta(\theta)\Phi(\phi)$ with $\Phi\propto e^{im\phi}$ and $\Theta(\theta)\propto P_l^{|m|}(\cos\theta)$. The overall constant is fixed by orthonormality on the unit sphere, $\int|Y|^2\,d\Omega=1$ with $d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$. The standard (physics) normalization, including the Condon-Shortley phase $(-1)^m$ for $m>0$, is

Spherical harmonics: simultaneous eigenfunctions of $L^2$ and $L_z$
$$\hat L^2 Y_l^m=\hbar^2 l(l+1)Y_l^m,\qquad \hat L_z Y_l^m=\hbar m\,Y_l^m$$ $$Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)=(-1)^m\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}}\; P_l^m(\cos\theta)\,e^{im\phi}$$ $$l=0,1,2,\ldots;\qquad m=-l,-l+1,\ldots,l$$

The factor $\sqrt{(2l+1)/4\pi}$ comes from the $\theta$ integral of $[P_l(\cos\theta)]^2$; the ratio of factorials adjusts the $m$-dependent norm of $P_l^m$. Explicit low-$l$ cases (often the ones drawn by orbital3d.py):

$$Y_0^0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}},\qquad Y_1^0=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\cos\theta,\qquad Y_1^{\pm1}=\mp\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\sin\theta\,e^{\pm i\phi}$$ $$Y_2^0=\sqrt{\frac{5}{16\pi}}(3\cos^2\theta-1),\qquad Y_2^{\pm1}=\mp\sqrt{\frac{15}{8\pi}}\sin\theta\cos\theta\,e^{\pm i\phi},\qquad Y_2^{\pm2}=\sqrt{\frac{15}{32\pi}}\sin^2\theta\,e^{\pm 2i\phi}$$

Spherical harmonics form a complete orthonormal set on the unit sphere:

$$\int Y_{l'}^{m'*}(\theta,\phi)\,Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)\,d\Omega =\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}$$

Completeness means any square-integrable angular function can be expanded as $\sum_{l,m}c_{lm}Y_l^m$. Orthonormality is what later collapses angular integrals in transition-matrix elements to selection rules (Wigner-Eckart theorem, subsection E) instead of a numerical quadrature every time. For each fixed $l$ there are $2l+1$ allowed $m$ values; that counting is the geometric origin of the $(2l+1)$-fold $m$-degeneracy of any purely central potential.

Step 5 (radial). Put $\Lambda=l(l+1)$ back into the $r$-only side of the separated equation and rearrange:

Radial Schrödinger equation
$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right)+\left[\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}+V(r)\right]R(r)=E\,R(r)$$

Step 6 (reduced radial function). The substitution $u(r)\equiv rR(r)$ removes the first-derivative term. With $R=u/r$,

$$r^2\frac{dR}{dr}=r u'-u,\qquad \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right)=r u'',\qquad \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right)=\frac{u''}{r}$$

Substitute into the radial equation and multiply through by $r$:

Reduced radial equation: the working form used throughout
$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2}+V_{\rm eff}(r)\,u(r)=E\,u(r),\qquad V_{\rm eff}(r)=V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2\,l(l+1)}{2mr^2}$$

with boundary condition $u(0)=0$ (needed for $\psi=u/r$ to remain finite at the origin) and $u(r)\to0$ as $r\to\infty$ for a bound state. The extra term, the centrifugal barrier, grows as $r^{-2}$ and, for any $l>0$, pushes the classically allowed region away from $r=0$.

🔗

Used in: this is the exact reason the quantum defect $\delta_l$ shrinks toward zero at high $l$ (Section 02, rydberg.py): a large centrifugal barrier keeps high-$l$ electrons entirely outside the ionic core, where $V(r)=-e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 r$ is purely Coulombic, so the atom behaves exactly like hydrogen. It is also the literal potential solved numerically inside orbital3d.py's radial grid.

B. The Hydrogen Atom: Exact Solution

Set $V(r)=-\dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ ($Z=1$ for hydrogen; kept general since the same solution describes any hydrogenic ion, and via the quantum defect approximately describes the alkali valence electron far from the core). Start from the reduced radial equation of subsection A with this $V$.

Nondimensionalization (explicit)

For a bound state $E<0$, define $\kappa\equiv\sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar>0$ so that $E=-\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$, set $\rho\equiv\kappa r$, and introduce

$$\lambda\equiv\frac{mZe^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2\kappa}$$

Start from the reduced radial equation with $V=-Ze^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} +\left[-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}+\frac{\hbar^2 l(l+1)}{2m r^2}\right]u =E\,u$$

Chain rule: $du/dr=\kappa\,du/d\rho$, so $d^2u/dr^2=\kappa^2\,d^2u/d\rho^2$. Multiply the ODE through by $2m/\hbar^2$ and rearrange:

$$\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} =\left[\frac{2m|E|}{\hbar^2} -\frac{2mZe^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2\,r} +\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}\right]u$$

Replace $2m|E|/\hbar^2=\kappa^2$, divide by $\kappa^2$, and substitute $r=\rho/\kappa$. The Coulomb coefficient becomes $2mZe^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2\kappa)=2\lambda$, giving the dimensionless equation

$$\frac{d^2u}{d\rho^2} =\left[1-\frac{2\lambda}{\rho}+\frac{l(l+1)}{\rho^2}\right]u$$

Asymptotic behavior fixes the form of the solution. As $\rho\to\infty$, the equation reduces to $u''\approx u$, so $u\sim e^{\pm\rho}$; normalizability selects $u\sim e^{-\rho}$. As $\rho\to0$, the $l(l+1)/\rho^2$ term dominates, giving the indicial equation $u''\approx l(l+1)u/\rho^2$, solved by $u\sim\rho^{l+1}$ (the regular solution; $\rho^{-l}$ is rejected since it diverges at the origin for $l>0$ and is not normalizable even for $l=0$). This motivates the exact ansatz

$$u(\rho)=\rho^{l+1}e^{-\rho}\,v(\rho)$$

From the ansatz to Kummer's equation (explicit)

Write $u=\rho^{l+1}e^{-\rho}v$. First derivative (product rule on the three factors):

$$u' =e^{-\rho}\rho^{l}\Bigl[ (l+1-\rho)\,v+\rho\,v' \Bigr] \equiv e^{-\rho}\rho^{l}\,w,\qquad w\equiv(l+1-\rho)v+\rho\,v'$$

Differentiate again. With $w'=-v+(l+2-\rho)v'+\rho\,v''$ and $u'=e^{-\rho}\rho^{l}w$,

$$u'' =e^{-\rho}\rho^{l-1}\Bigl[ (l-\rho)\,w+\rho\,w' \Bigr] =e^{-\rho}\rho^{l-1}\Bigl[ \rho^2 v'' +2\rho(l+1-\rho)\,v' +\bigl((l-\rho)(l+1-\rho)-\rho\bigr)v \Bigr]$$

The differential equation requires $u''=[1-2\lambda/\rho+l(l+1)/\rho^2]u$. The right-hand side is

$$\left[1-\frac{2\lambda}{\rho}+\frac{l(l+1)}{\rho^2}\right]u =e^{-\rho}\rho^{l-1}\bigl[\rho^2-2\lambda\rho+l(l+1)\bigr]v$$

Cancel the common never-zero factor $e^{-\rho}\rho^{l-1}$, move all terms to one side, and expand the $v$ coefficient:

$$(l-\rho)(l+1-\rho)-\rho-\rho^2+2\lambda\rho-l(l+1) =2\rho(\lambda-l-1)$$

What remains is $\rho^2 v''+2\rho(l+1-\rho)v'+2\rho(\lambda-l-1)v=0$. Divide by $\rho>0$:

Kummer (confluent hypergeometric) equation for $v$
$$\rho\,v''+2(l+1-\rho)\,v'-2(l+1-\lambda)\,v=0$$

(Equivalent textbook form: if one instead sets $\rho_{\mathrm{std}}=2\kappa r$, the same physics yields $\rho\,v''+(2l+2-\rho)v'-(l+1-\lambda)v=0$ with asymptotic factor $e^{-\rho/2}$. Here $\rho=\kappa r$ keeps the asymptotic factor $e^{-\rho}$.)

Power series for $v$: recursion and truncation

Insert $v=\sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k\rho^k$ ($c_0\neq0$) into Kummer's equation. Term by term:

$$v'=\sum_{k=1}^\infty k c_k\rho^{k-1},\qquad v''=\sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1)c_k\rho^{k-2}$$ $$\rho\,v''=\sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1)c_k\rho^{k-1} =\sum_{j=1}^\infty (j+1)j\,c_{j+1}\,\rho^{j}$$ $$2(l+1)v'=\sum_{k=1}^\infty 2(l+1)k\,c_k\rho^{k-1} =\sum_{j=0}^\infty 2(l+1)(j+1)c_{j+1}\,\rho^{j}$$ $$-2\rho\,v'=-2\sum_{k=1}^\infty k c_k\rho^{k},\qquad -2(l+1-\lambda)v=-2(l+1-\lambda)\sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k\rho^{k}$$

Align every sum on $\rho^k$, equate the coefficient of each power to zero, and solve for $c_{k+1}$:

$$c_{k+1}=\frac{2(k+l+1-\lambda)}{(k+1)(k+2l+2)}\,c_k$$

For large $k$, $c_{k+1}/c_k\sim 2/k$, which is the same ratio as the series for $e^{2\rho}$. An unterminated $v$ therefore grows as $e^{2\rho}$, cancels the built-in $e^{-\rho}$ in $u$, and leaves a non-normalizable $u\sim e^{+\rho}$. The only cure is termination at finite degree $k=n_r$ ($n_r=0,1,2,\ldots$), which forces the numerator to vanish at $k=n_r$:

Quantization condition: where "n" comes from
$$\lambda = n_r+l+1 \equiv n \in \{1,2,3,\ldots\}$$

Since $\lambda$ was defined in terms of $E$ through $\kappa$, this condition is the energy quantization. Unwinding explicitly: $\lambda=n$ means $\kappa=mZe^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2 n)=Z/(na_0)$, and $E=-\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$ becomes

Hydrogenic energy levels
$$E_n=-\frac{m Z^2e^4}{2(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2\,n^2}=-\frac{Z^2\,{\rm Ry}}{n^2},\qquad {\rm Ry}\equiv\frac{me^4}{2(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2}=13.6057\,{\rm eV}$$

This is exactly the constant Ry = 13.6057 hardcoded at the top of rydberg.py. With $\lambda=n$ the terminating polynomial $v$ is (up to a constant) an associated Laguerre polynomial. Matching the standard argument $x=2\rho=2\kappa r=2Zr/(na_0)$ gives $v(\rho)\propto L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}(2\rho)$, and restoring $R=u/r$ with the usual $L^2$ normalization produces

Exact hydrogenic radial wavefunction
$$R_{nl}(r)=-\sqrt{\left(\frac{2Z}{na_0}\right)^3\frac{(n-l-1)!}{2n[(n+l)!]^3}}\; e^{-Zr/na_0}\left(\frac{2Zr}{na_0}\right)^{l} L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}\!\left(\frac{2Zr}{na_0}\right)$$

The full bound state is then $\psi_{nlm}(\mathbf r)=R_{nl}(r)\,Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)$, with $n=1,2,\ldots$, $l=0,\ldots,n-1$, and $m=-l,\ldots,l$. The ground state ($n=1,l=0,m=0$, $Z=1$) collapses to the elementary form

$$\psi_{100}(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}\,e^{-r/a_0},\qquad R_{10}(r)=2a_0^{-3/2}e^{-r/a_0}$$

The associated Laguerre polynomials: definition and recurrence

Associated Laguerre polynomials $L_k^\alpha(x)$ are the polynomial solutions of

$$x\,y''+(\alpha+1-x)y'+k\,y=0$$

(Kummer's equation above is this ODE after the change of argument $x=2\rho$ and the identification $k=n-l-1$, $\alpha=2l+1$). They admit the explicit sum

$$L_k^\alpha(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^j\binom{k+\alpha}{k-j}\frac{x^j}{j!}$$

and the three-term recurrence used by orbital3d.py:

Laguerre three-term recurrence
$$L_0^\alpha(x)=1,\qquad L_1^\alpha(x)=1+\alpha-x$$ $$k\,L_k^\alpha(x) = (2k-1+\alpha-x)\,L_{k-1}^\alpha(x) - (k-1+\alpha)\,L_{k-2}^\alpha(x)$$

Low-degree checks: $L_0^1=1$, $L_1^1=2-x$, $L_2^1=\tfrac12(x^2-6x+6)$, which recover $R_{21}$ and $R_{20}$ when inserted into the formula for $R_{nl}$.

✅

This is, term for term, the recurrence implemented in orbital3d.py's in-browser Web Worker (its lag(p, alpha, x) function). Verified numerically against SciPy's reference associated-Laguerre implementation across several $(n,l)$ combinations before writing this section: agreement at the $10^{-13}$-$10^{-15}$ level (machine precision) in every case. The overflow-safe rescaling the code performs when intermediate values exceed $10^{100}$ does not touch the underlying recursion; it only tracks and re-applies a running log-scale factor, which is why the numbers match exactly.

Degeneracy: and why real atoms need a quantum defect at all

Notice $E_n$ depends only on $n=n_r+l+1$, not on $l$ or $m$ separately. For fixed $n$, $l$ ranges over $0,1,\ldots,n-1$ and $m$ over $-l,\ldots,l$, giving $n^2$ degenerate states at every energy level. This is more degeneracy than the spherical symmetry of $V(r)$ alone guarantees (spherical symmetry only requires degeneracy across $m$, at fixed $l$); the extra $l$-degeneracy is a special, "accidental" feature of the exact $1/r$ Coulomb potential, reflecting a hidden dynamical symmetry (the conserved Runge-Lenz vector, generating an $SO(4)$ symmetry group beyond ordinary $SO(3)$ rotations) that exists only for this specific potential.

🔗

Used in: the moment the ionic core distorts the pure $-e^2/r$ tail at small $r$ (any real atom beyond hydrogen), this accidental degeneracy is lifted and the energy starts depending on $l$. Quantum Defect Theory (Section 02) packages that entire $l$-dependence into a single number $\delta_l$ via $E_{n,l}=-{\rm Ry}/(n-\delta_l)^2$, i.e. $\delta_l$ is a direct, quantitative measure of how far a given orbital's energy has been pushed away from this exact hydrogenic degeneracy by core penetration.

Worked check: ground state

$n=1,l=0,Z=1$: $E_1=-13.6057$ eV (the ionization energy of atomic hydrogen, matching the CODATA value to the precision quoted). Bohr radius $a_0=4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/me^2=0.529177$ Å falls out of the same definitions used above ($\kappa^{-1}$ at $n=1,Z=1$).

Radial nodes

$L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}$ is a polynomial of degree $n-l-1$, so $R_{nl}$ has exactly $n-l-1$ radial nodes (zero crossings, excluding the origin and infinity), this is the "shell structure" visible directly in orbital3d.py's isosurface renders as nested shells at different isovalues.

C. Time-Independent Perturbation Theory

Suppose $H=H^0+\lambda V$, where $H^0$ has known eigenstates $H^0|n^0\rangle=E_n^0|n^0\rangle$, $V$ is some added interaction (e.g. a static electric field), and $\lambda$ is a formal bookkeeping parameter (set to 1 at the end) used only to organize the expansion order by order. Expand both the exact eigenstate and eigenvalue in powers of $\lambda$:

$$E_n=E_n^0+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2E_n^{(2)}+\cdots,\qquad |n\rangle=|n^0\rangle+\lambda|n^{(1)}\rangle+\lambda^2|n^{(2)}\rangle+\cdots$$

Insert into $H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$ and collect terms order by order in $\lambda$:

$$\lambda^0:\quad H^0|n^0\rangle=E_n^0|n^0\rangle \quad\text{(trivially satisfied)}$$ $$\lambda^1:\quad H^0|n^{(1)}\rangle+V|n^0\rangle = E_n^0|n^{(1)}\rangle+E_n^{(1)}|n^0\rangle$$

Project the $\lambda^1$ equation onto $\langle n^0|$. Since $\langle n^0|H^0=E_n^0\langle n^0|$, the term $\langle n^0|H^0|n^{(1)}\rangle$ cancels exactly against $E_n^0\langle n^0|n^{(1)}\rangle$ on the right, leaving:

First-order energy shift
$$E_n^{(1)}=\langle n^0|V|n^0\rangle$$

To find the first-order state correction, expand $|n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\ne n}c_m|m^0\rangle$ (no $|n^0\rangle$ component needed; that freedom is absorbed into normalization) and project the $\lambda^1$ equation onto $\langle m^0|$ for $m\ne n$: $E_m^0 c_m + \langle m^0|V|n^0\rangle = E_n^0 c_m$, so

$$c_m=\frac{\langle m^0|V|n^0\rangle}{E_n^0-E_m^0}\quad\Rightarrow\quad |n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{\langle m^0|V|n^0\rangle}{E_n^0-E_m^0}\,|m^0\rangle$$

Now the $\lambda^2$ order:

$$\lambda^2:\quad H^0|n^{(2)}\rangle+V|n^{(1)}\rangle=E_n^0|n^{(2)}\rangle+E_n^{(1)}|n^{(1)}\rangle+E_n^{(2)}|n^0\rangle$$

Project onto $\langle n^0|$ again; the $H^0|n^{(2)}\rangle$ and $E_n^0|n^{(2)}\rangle$ terms cancel as before, and $\langle n^0|E_n^{(1)}|n^{(1)}\rangle=E_n^{(1)}\langle n^0|n^{(1)}\rangle=0$ (by the orthogonality choice made above), leaving $\langle n^0|V|n^{(1)}\rangle=E_n^{(2)}$. Substituting the expression for $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ just derived:

Second-order energy shift: the origin of the static polarizability formula
$$E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|\langle m^0|V|n^0\rangle|^2}{E_n^0-E_m^0}$$

Application: static electric polarizability

Take $V=-\hat{\mathbf d}\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\,\mathcal E$, the interaction of the atom's dipole operator with a static field of magnitude $\mathcal E$ along direction $\varepsilon$. By definition, the static scalar polarizability $\alpha(0)$ is the coefficient in $\Delta E=-\tfrac12\alpha(0)\mathcal E^2$. Matching this to the second-order result above (with $V^2\propto\mathcal E^2$ already factored out):

Static polarizability from 2nd-order PT
$$\alpha(0)=2\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|\langle m|\hat d_\varepsilon|n\rangle|^2}{E_m-E_n}$$
🔗

Used in: converting the dipole matrix element to oscillator strength (derived in subsection D below) turns this exactly into alpha_real(0, transitions) in polarizability.py: the $\omega\to0$ limit of the dynamic formula used throughout that script and in blackbody.py's static BBR shift.

Degenerate perturbation theory

The derivation above silently assumed $E_n^0$ is non-degenerate; dividing by $E_n^0-E_m^0$ is meaningless if some $m\ne n$ shares the same unperturbed energy. When several states $\{|n_1^0\rangle,\ldots,|n_g^0\rangle\}$ are degenerate at energy $E_n^0$, expand in the same way but project the $\lambda^1$ equation onto the whole degenerate subspace. Writing $|n\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^g a_j|n_j^0\rangle+\lambda|n^{(1)}\rangle$ and projecting onto $\langle n_i^0|$ gives the matrix equation

$$\sum_{j=1}^g a_j\,\langle n_i^0|V|n_j^0\rangle = E_n^{(1)} a_i,\qquad \mathbf V^{(0)} \mathbf a = E_n^{(1)}\mathbf a$$

Nonzero solutions exist only when $E_n^{(1)}$ is an eigenvalue of the $g\times g$ matrix $V_{ij}=\langle n_i^0|V|n_j^0\rangle$. The correct first-order energy corrections are therefore the roots of the secular equation $\det(\mathbf V-E^{(1)}\mathbf I)=0$, and the corresponding eigenvectors $\mathbf a$ define the proper zeroth-order combinations $|n\rangle^{(0)}=\sum_j a_j|n_j^0\rangle$ to which the non-degenerate formulas for $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ and $E_n^{(2)}$ may then be applied.

🔗

Used in: this is exactly what hyperfine.py's Breit-Rabi calculation does: the hyperfine + Zeeman Hamiltonian is diagonalized numerically within the full $(2I+1)(2J+1)$-dimensional $|m_I,m_J\rangle$ degenerate subspace at each magnetic field value, rather than using a non-degenerate perturbative formula.

D. Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, the TRK Sum Rule, and Dynamic Polarizability

Now let $H(t)=H^0+V(t)$, with $V(t)$ switched on at $t=0$. Move to the interaction picture, $|\psi_I(t)\rangle=e^{iH^0t/\hbar}|\psi_S(t)\rangle$, under which the Schrödinger equation becomes

$$i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi_I(t)\rangle=V_I(t)|\psi_I(t)\rangle,\qquad V_I(t)\equiv e^{iH^0t/\hbar}V(t)e^{-iH^0t/\hbar}$$

Integrating formally and iterating once (first-order Dyson series), starting in state $|i\rangle$ at $t=0$:

$$|\psi_I(t)\rangle \approx |i\rangle - \frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t V_I(t')\,|i\rangle\,dt'$$

The amplitude to be found in a different state $|f\rangle$ at time $t$ is $c_f(t)=\langle f|\psi_I(t)\rangle= -\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^tV_{fi}(t')\,e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}dt'$, where $V_{fi}\equiv\langle f|V|i\rangle$.

Case 1: constant perturbation: Fermi's Golden Rule

For $V(t)=V$ constant (switched on at $t=0$, time-independent thereafter):

$$c_f(t) = -\frac{iV_{fi}}{\hbar}\int_0^t e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}dt' = -\frac{V_{fi}}{\hbar\omega_{fi}}\big(e^{i\omega_{fi}t}-1\big)$$ $$|c_f(t)|^2 = \frac{|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\big(2-2\cos\omega_{fi}t\big)=\frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\omega_{fi}t}{2}\right)$$

As a function of $\omega_{fi}$, this is a sinc$^2$-type function centered at $\omega_{fi}=0$ with height $\propto t^2$ and width $\propto1/t$; its area is independent of $t$ (using $\int_{-\infty}^\infty \sin^2(xt/2)/x^2\,dx=\pi t/2$):

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|c_f(t)|^2\,d\omega_{fi}=\frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{\pi t}{2}=\frac{2\pi t}{\hbar^2}|V_{fi}|^2$$

As $t\to\infty$ this function of $\omega_{fi}$ becomes infinitely tall and infinitely narrow while preserving that fixed area, the defining property of a Dirac delta function, specifically $\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\sin^2(\omega t/2)}{\omega^2}=\frac{\pi t}{2}\delta(\omega)$. For transitions into a continuum of final states with density of states $\rho(E_f)$, summing $|c_f(t)|^2$ over that continuum and dividing by $t$ gives a constant, time-independent transition rate:

Fermi's Golden Rule
$$\Gamma_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\,\rho(E_f)$$

Case 2: oscillating perturbation: two resonance denominators

Now take $V(t)=V_0\cos(\omega t)=\tfrac12 V_0\big(e^{i\omega t}+e^{-i\omega t}\big)$, a monochromatic drive at frequency $\omega$, the relevant case for both light absorption/emission and the AC Stark effect. Repeating the same first-order integral, each exponential term in $V(t)$ produces its own resonance:

$$c_f(t) = -\frac{i(V_0)_{fi}}{2\hbar}\left[\frac{e^{i(\omega_{fi}+\omega)t}-1}{i(\omega_{fi}+\omega)}+\frac{e^{i(\omega_{fi}-\omega)t}-1}{i(\omega_{fi}-\omega)}\right]$$

One term peaks when $\omega=-\omega_{fi}$ (stimulated emission-type resonance), the other when $\omega=\omega_{fi}$ (absorption-type resonance), precisely the two-denominator structure that appears in every dynamic-response formula below. This calculation, carried through to the resulting transition rate at exact resonance, is the microscopic derivation of the stimulated absorption/emission rates behind the Einstein $B$ coefficients (Section 06). At exact absorption resonance ($\omega=\omega_{fi}>0$), the second denominator dominates and $|c_f(t)|^2\approx |V_{fi}|^2 t^2/(4\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2)$ for short times, giving a transition rate $W_{i\to f}=|V_{fi}|^2\pi/(\hbar^2\omega_{fi})$ after the same $\sin^2$ integration used above. For an electric-dipole coupling $V_{fi}=-\tfrac12\langle f|\hat d|i\rangle\mathcal E_0$, this is the Einstein $B$ coefficient (stimulated absorption) once the field energy density is related to $\mathcal E_0$.

The Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn (TRK) sum rule

Before converting the dynamic polarizability to oscillator strengths, establish the normalization used throughout the pipeline. Define, for one Cartesian direction, the (dimensionless) oscillator strength for a transition $n\to m$:

$$f_{nm}\equiv\frac{2m_e\omega_{mn}}{\hbar}\,|\langle m|\hat x|n\rangle|^2$$

Start from the commutator identity $[H^0,\hat x]=\dfrac{-i\hbar}{m_e}\hat p_x$ (direct consequence of $[\hat p^2,\hat x]=-2i\hbar\hat p_x$). Taking the matrix element between eigenstates of $H^0$: since $H^0$ acting to the left on $\langle m|$ gives $E_m$ and acting to the right on $|n\rangle$ gives $E_n$,

$$\langle m|[H^0,\hat x]|n\rangle=(E_m-E_n)\langle m|\hat x|n\rangle=-\frac{i\hbar}{m_e}\langle m|\hat p_x|n\rangle$$

Now evaluate the double commutator $S\equiv\langle n|[\hat x,[H^0,\hat x]]|n\rangle$ two different ways. Directly, using $[\hat x,\hat p_x]=i\hbar$:

$$[\hat x,[H^0,\hat x]]=\left[\hat x,\,-\frac{i\hbar}{m_e}\hat p_x\right]=-\frac{i\hbar}{m_e}[\hat x,\hat p_x]=-\frac{i\hbar}{m_e}(i\hbar)=\frac{\hbar^2}{m_e} \quad\Rightarrow\quad S=\frac{\hbar^2}{m_e}$$

Via completeness ($\sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|=1$), writing $C\equiv[H^0,\hat x]$:

$$S=\langle n|\hat xC|n\rangle-\langle n|C\hat x|n\rangle =\sum_m\Big[\langle n|\hat x|m\rangle\langle m|C|n\rangle-\langle n|C|m\rangle\langle m|\hat x|n\rangle\Big]$$

Using $\langle m|C|n\rangle=(E_m-E_n)\langle m|\hat x|n\rangle$ and its conjugate relation for the second term, and noting $|\langle n|\hat x|m\rangle|^2=|\langle m|\hat x|n\rangle|^2$, both terms combine to give $S=2\sum_m(E_m-E_n)|\langle m|\hat x|n\rangle|^2$. Equating the two evaluations of $S$:

Sum rule (pre-normalization form)
$$\sum_m (E_m-E_n)\,|\langle m|\hat x|n\rangle|^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}$$

Multiplying both sides by $2m_e/\hbar^2$ and recognizing the definition of $f_{nm}$:

Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rule
$$\sum_m f_{nm}=1 \quad\text{(one electron, one Cartesian axis)}$$

For an isotropic 3D problem the same result holds with the standard $f_{nm}=\frac{2m_e\omega_{mn}}{3\hbar}|\langle m|\mathbf r|n\rangle|^2$ definition (dividing the 3D dipole matrix element equally among $x,y,z$), and for $N_e$ active electrons the sum rule generalizes to $\sum_m f_{nm}=N_e$, the normalization convention used for fosc throughout lifetimes.py and polarizability.py.

✅

Substituting $f_{nm}=(2m_e/\hbar)\omega_{mn}|\langle m|x|n\rangle|^2$ into $\sum_m f_{nm}$ and using $S=\hbar^2/2m_e$ above reduces the sum to exactly $1$.

Dynamic polarizability: full derivation

Take $V(t)=-\hat d_\varepsilon\,\mathcal E_0\cos(\omega t)$ and work to first order in the field. From Case 2, the amplitude of state $|m\rangle$ (with $m\ne n$) is, after integrating and keeping the near-resonant term ($\omega\approx\omega_{mn}= (E_m-E_n)/\hbar$):

$$c_m(t)\approx -\frac{\langle m|\hat d_\varepsilon|n\rangle\,\mathcal E_0}{2\hbar(\omega_{mn}-\omega)}\,\big(e^{i(\omega_{mn}-\omega)t}-1\big)$$

The induced dipole expectation value is $\langle\hat d_\varepsilon\rangle=\langle n|\hat d_\varepsilon|n\rangle +2\,\mathrm{Re}\sum_{m\ne n}\langle n|\hat d_\varepsilon|m\rangle\,c_m$ (the factor 2 comes from combining $c_m$ and $c_m^*$). In the steady oscillating part proportional to $e^{-i\omega t}$:

$$\langle\hat d_\varepsilon\rangle_{\rm ac} =\mathcal E_0\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{2\omega_{mn}}{\hbar(\omega_{mn}^2-\omega^2)}\, |\langle m|\hat d_\varepsilon|n\rangle|^2\,e^{-i\omega t}$$

By definition the dynamic polarizability satisfies $\langle\hat d_\varepsilon\rangle_{\rm ac}=\alpha(\omega)\,\mathcal E_0\,e^{-i\omega t}$, so comparing coefficients:

Dynamic polarizability: sum-over-states (Kramers-Heisenberg-type) formula
$$\alpha(\omega)=\frac{2}{\hbar}\sum_{m\ne n}\omega_{mn}\,\frac{|\langle m|\hat d_\varepsilon|n\rangle|^2}{\omega_{mn}^2-\omega^2}$$

Converting the dipole matrix element to oscillator strength via the TRK definition ($|\langle m|\hat d_\varepsilon|n\rangle|^2 = \hbar f_{nm}/(2m_e\omega_{mn})$ in one Cartesian direction; atomic units $m_e=\hbar=1$):

Atomic-unit form: exactly alpha_real() in the pipeline
$$\alpha(\omega)=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{f_{nm}}{\omega_{mn}^2-\omega^2}$$
🔗

This is, term for term, alpha_real(omega_au, transitions) in polarizability.py and blackbody.py: the code's sign field (+1 for a transition upward from the state of interest, -1 for downward) is exactly the physical statement that $\omega_{mn}$ flips sign depending on whether $m$ lies above or below $n$; a downward-lying $m$ contributes with $\omega_{mn}<0$, which the code implements explicitly rather than relying on the formula's sign to work itself out silently. Setting $\omega=0$ recovers exactly the static formula from subsection C.

E. Angular Momentum Coupling and the Wigner-Eckart Theorem

Any angular momentum operator $\mathbf J$ (orbital $\mathbf L$, spin $\mathbf S$, nuclear spin $\mathbf I$, or a sum of these) is defined by its commutation algebra:

$$[\hat J_x,\hat J_y]=i\hbar\hat J_z\ \text{(cyclic)},\qquad \hat J_\pm\equiv\hat J_x\pm i\hat J_y$$

Eigenstates $|j,m\rangle$ satisfy $\hat J^2|jm\rangle=\hbar^2j(j+1)|jm\rangle$, $\hat J_z|jm\rangle=\hbar m|jm\rangle$. The ladder-operator normalization follows from $\hat J_\mp\hat J_\pm=\hat J^2-\hat J_z^2\mp\hbar\hat J_z$: taking $\langle jm|\hat J_\mp\hat J_\pm|jm\rangle=\|\hat J_\pm|jm\rangle\|^2\ge0$ and evaluating the right-hand side operator in the eigenbasis gives

$$\hat J_\pm|jm\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}\;|j,m\pm1\rangle$$

Addition of two angular momenta

Given two commuting angular momenta $\mathbf J_1,\mathbf J_2$ (acting on different degrees of freedom (e.g. orbital and spin, or electronic and nuclear), the total $\mathbf J=\mathbf J_1+\mathbf J_2$ is also a good angular momentum operator, but the uncoupled product states $|j_1m_1\rangle|j_2m_2\rangle$ are not eigenstates of $\hat J^2$ (only of $\hat J_z=\hat J_{1z}+\hat J_{2z}$, trivially, since $m=m_1+m_2$). The coupled eigenstates are linear combinations, defining the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients:

$$|j,m\rangle=\sum_{m_1,m_2}\langle j_1m_1;j_2m_2|jm\rangle\;|j_1m_1\rangle|j_2m_2\rangle$$

Two selection rules on these coefficients follow directly: $m=m_1+m_2$ (immediate from the $\hat J_z$ eigenvalue), and the triangle rule $|j_1-j_2|\le j\le j_1+j_2$. The triangle rule can be checked by simple dimension counting: the uncoupled basis has $(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)$ states total, and the coupled basis must have exactly the same total dimension: $\sum_{j=|j_1-j_2|}^{j_1+j_2}(2j+1)=(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)$ is a standard finite-sum identity, so the triangle rule is precisely the range that makes the counting close.

🔗

Used in: hyperfine coupling $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf J$ (hyperfine.py's F_levels() function returns exactly the triangle-rule range $|I-J|,\ldots,I+J$), and the $L$-$S$ coupling implicit whenever ORCA reports a state's total spin quantum number.

Spherical tensor operators and the Wigner-Eckart theorem

An operator $T^k_q$ ($q=-k,\ldots,k$) is a spherical tensor operator of rank $k$ if it transforms under rotations exactly like the angular momentum eigenstate $|k,q\rangle$, equivalently if it obeys the same commutation relations with $\hat J$ that $|k,q\rangle$ satisfies as an eigenstate:

$$[\hat J_z,T^k_q]=\hbar q\,T^k_q,\qquad [\hat J_\pm,T^k_q]=\hbar\sqrt{k(k+1)-q(q\pm1)}\;T^k_{q\pm1}$$

This is a purely algebraic definition, with no reference to any specific physical operator. Because $T^k_q$ acting on a state changes its angular momentum in exactly the same way a state $|kq\rangle$ would if "added" to it (via the same Clebsch-Gordan coefficients that couple two angular momenta), matrix elements of $T^k_q$ factor cleanly:

Wigner-Eckart theorem
$$\langle \alpha'j'm'|T^k_q|\alpha jm\rangle =\langle jm;kq|j'm'\rangle\;\frac{\langle \alpha'j'\|T^k\|\alpha j\rangle}{\sqrt{2j'+1}}$$

All dependence on the orientation quantum numbers $m,m',q$ (i.e. all the "geometry", sits entirely in the Clebsch-Gordan coefficient, which is known in closed form and vanishes outside the triangle rule $|j-k|\le j'\le j+k$ and unless $m'=m+q$. All the "physics" (radial integrals, dynamics, atomic-structure detail) sits in the single reduced matrix element $\langle\alpha'j'\|T^k\|\alpha j\rangle$, independent of $m,m',q$ entirely. This is the rigorous machinery behind selection rules: a transition is allowed only if the corresponding Clebsch-Gordan coefficient is nonzero, full stop; no dynamical calculation is needed to know that.

Application: E1 selection rules, derived rather than asserted

The electric dipole operator $\hat{\mathbf d}=-e\hat{\mathbf r}$ is a rank-1 spherical tensor (its three spherical components $d_{1,0}=\hat z$, $d_{1,\pm1}=\mp(\hat x\pm i\hat y)/\sqrt2$ transform exactly like $Y_1^q$, by direct construction). Apply Wigner-Eckart with $k=1$:

$$\langle n'l'j'm'|\hat d_q|nljm\rangle=\langle jm;1q|j'm'\rangle\,\langle n'l'j'\|\hat d\|nlj\rangle/\sqrt{2j'+1}$$

$\Delta J=0,\pm1$: the triangle rule for $k=1$ forces $|j-1|\le j'\le j+1$, i.e. $j'\in\{j-1,j,j+1\}$, directly $\Delta J=0,\pm1$.

$J=0\to J=0$ forbidden: if $j=0$, only $m=0$ exists, and the triangle rule with $j=0,k=1$ forces $j'=1$ exactly (the range $|0-1|\le j'\le0+1$ contains only $j'=1$); a $J=0$ state can connect only to $J'=1$ states via a rank-1 tensor, which means $J=0\to J=0$ is not merely disfavored but strictly forbidden by angular-momentum algebra alone.

$\Delta l=\pm1$: this rule needs one more ingredient, beyond Wigner-Eckart in $J$: parity. Central-potential eigenstates $|nlm\rangle$ have definite parity $(-1)^l$ (spherical harmonics satisfy $Y_l^m(\pi-\theta,\phi+\pi)=(-1)^lY_l^m(\theta,\phi)$). The position operator $\hat{\mathbf r}$ is parity-odd ($\hat{\mathbf r}\to-\hat{\mathbf r}$ under inversion), so $\langle n'l'|\hat{\mathbf r}|nl\rangle$ vanishes unless $(-1)^{l'}\cdot(-1)\cdot(-1)^l=+1$, i.e. $l+l'$ must be odd. Separately, $\hat{\mathbf r}$ is also a rank-1 spherical tensor in orbital angular momentum specifically, so the same triangle-rule argument applied to $l$ (rather than $j$) gives $|l-1|\le l'\le l+1$. The only value of $l'$ satisfying both "$l+l'$ odd" and "$l'\in\{l-1,l,l+1\}$" is $l'=l\pm1$ ($l'=l$ is even relative to $l$, so it is excluded by parity even though it survives the triangle rule alone), giving exactly $\Delta l=\pm1$.

✅

Used in: this is the justification for the selection rules transitions.py's j_selection_rule() enforces - a consequence of representation theory plus parity.

F. The Density Matrix and Optical Bloch Equations

A driven atom that can spontaneously decay is an open quantum system: it exchanges energy (via emitted photons) with an environment (the vacuum electromagnetic field) that is not being tracked explicitly. Pure-state Schrödinger evolution cannot describe irreversible decay on its own; the density-matrix formalism is the minimal extension that can.

For a statistical ensemble in which the system is in state $|\psi_i\rangle$ with probability $p_i$, define the density operator $\hat\rho\equiv\sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$. It satisfies $\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat\rho=1$, $\hat\rho=\hat\rho^\dagger$, and expectation values are computed as $\langle A\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat\rho A)$. Differentiating $\hat\rho$ using the Schrödinger equation for each $|\psi_i\rangle$:

$$i\hbar\frac{d\hat\rho}{dt}=\sum_ip_i\Big(i\hbar\tfrac{d}{dt}|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|+|\psi_i\rangle i\hbar\tfrac{d}{dt}\langle\psi_i|\Big) =\sum_ip_i\big(H|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|-|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|H\big)=[H,\hat\rho]$$
Von Neumann equation
$$i\hbar\frac{d\hat\rho}{dt}=[H,\hat\rho]$$

This alone still conserves purity and cannot describe decay. Spontaneous emission follows from coupling the atom to the quantized radiation field. In the Wigner-Weisskopf treatment one writes $H=H_{\rm atom}+H_{\rm field}+H_{\rm int}$ with $H_{\rm int}=\sum_k g_k\big(\hat\sigma_+\hat a_k e^{i\omega_k t}+\hat\sigma_-\hat a_k^\dagger e^{-i\omega_k t}\big)$ and applies the Markov approximation (the field correlation time is short compared with atomic evolution). Tracing over the reservoir gives, for a two-level atom, the Lindblad master equation with decay rate

Wigner-Weisskopf decay rate (result used as input below)
$$\Gamma_{e\to g}=\frac{\omega_0^3|\mathbf d_{eg}|^2}{3\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3} \equiv A_{eg}$$

Section 06 derives the same $A$ independently from detailed balance against Planck's law, so the pipeline's $\Gamma$ and Einstein $A$ are mutually consistent. For a driven two-level atom $\{|g\rangle,|e\rangle\}$, the master equation is

$$\frac{d\hat\rho}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H',\hat\rho]+\Gamma\Big(\hat\sigma_-\hat\rho\hat\sigma_+-\tfrac12\{\hat\sigma_+\hat\sigma_-,\hat\rho\}\Big),\qquad \hat\sigma_-\equiv|g\rangle\langle e|$$

where, after moving to a frame rotating at the drive frequency $\omega_L$ with $U=e^{i\omega_L t|e\rangle\langle e|}$, the interaction Hamiltonian becomes $H_{\rm int}=-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E\cos(\omega_L t)$ and picks up fast oscillating terms $\propto e^{\pm i(\omega_L+\omega_0)t}$. The rotating-wave approximation drops those counter-rotating terms (valid when $|\Delta|\ll\omega_0$), leaving

$$H'=-\hbar\Delta|e\rangle\langle e|-\tfrac{\hbar\Omega}{2}(|e\rangle\langle g|+|g\rangle\langle e|), \qquad \Delta\equiv\omega_L-\omega_0,\quad \Omega\equiv d_{ge}\mathcal E_0/\hbar$$

Writing out the matrix elements of the master equation in the $\{|g\rangle,|e\rangle\}$ basis (populations $\rho_{ee}$, $\rho_{gg}=1-\rho_{ee}$, and coherence $\rho_{eg}=\rho_{ge}^*$) gives the optical Bloch equations:

Optical Bloch Equations (two-level atom, RWA)
$$\dot\rho_{ee} = \frac{i\Omega}{2}(\rho_{eg}-\rho_{ge})-\Gamma\rho_{ee}$$ $$\dot\rho_{eg} = \Big(i\Delta-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\Big)\rho_{eg}+\frac{i\Omega}{2}\big(2\rho_{ee}-1\big)$$

These follow directly from $\dot\rho_{ee}=\langle e|\dot{\hat\rho}|e\rangle$ and $\dot\rho_{eg}=\langle g|\dot{\hat\rho}|e\rangle$: the Hamiltonian part gives $-(i/\hbar)\langle e|[H',\hat\rho]|e\rangle$ and produces the Rabi coupling $\tfrac{i\Omega}{2}(\rho_{eg}-\rho_{ge})$; the Lindblad dissipator gives $-\Gamma\rho_{ee}$ for the excited-state population and $-( \Gamma/2)\rho_{eg}$ for the coherence decay.

Steady state: full algebra

Set both derivatives to zero. From the first equation, using $\rho_{eg}-\rho_{ge}=2i\,\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{eg})$:

$$0=\frac{i\Omega}{2}\big(2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,\rho_{eg}\big)-\Gamma\rho_{ee} \;\Rightarrow\;\rho_{ee}=-\frac{\Omega}{\Gamma}\,\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{eg})\qquad(i)$$

From the second equation:

$$\rho_{eg}=\frac{-\tfrac{i\Omega}{2}(2\rho_{ee}-1)}{i\Delta-\Gamma/2} =\frac{\tfrac{i\Omega}{2}(1-2\rho_{ee})}{i\Delta-\Gamma/2}$$

Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate $(-i\Delta-\Gamma/2)$; the denominator becomes real, $(\Gamma/2)^2+\Delta^2$, and expanding the numerator $i(-i\Delta-\Gamma/2)=\Delta-i\Gamma/2$ gives

$$\rho_{eg}=\frac{\Omega}{2}(1-2\rho_{ee})\,\frac{\Delta-i\Gamma/2}{\Delta^2+\Gamma^2/4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathrm{Im}(\rho_{eg})=-\frac{\Omega\Gamma}{4}\,\frac{1-2\rho_{ee}}{\Delta^2+\Gamma^2/4}$$

Substituting into (i):

$$\rho_{ee}=\frac{\Omega^2}{4}\cdot\frac{1-2\rho_{ee}}{\Delta^2+\Gamma^2/4} \;\Rightarrow\; \rho_{ee}\Big(\Delta^2+\tfrac{\Gamma^2}{4}+\tfrac{\Omega^2}{2}\Big)=\frac{\Omega^2}{4}$$
Steady-state excited-state population
$$\rho_{ee}^{\rm ss}=\frac{\Omega^2/4}{\Delta^2+\Gamma^2/4+\Omega^2/2}$$

Define the on-resonance saturation parameter $s\equiv 2\Omega^2/\Gamma^2$ (equivalently $s=I/I_{\rm sat}$, matching the definition used in compare_elements.py). Dividing numerator and denominator by $\Gamma^2/4$:

$$\rho_{ee}^{\rm ss}=\frac{\Omega^2/\Gamma^2}{1+2\Omega^2/\Gamma^2+(2\Delta/\Gamma)^2} =\frac{s/2}{1+s+(2\Delta/\Gamma)^2}$$

The photon scattering rate is the rate of spontaneous decay events, $R_{\rm scatt}=\Gamma\rho_{ee}^{\rm ss}$ (each decay emits exactly one photon):

Photon scattering rate: derived, not merely stated
$$R_{\rm scatt}(I,\Delta)=\Gamma\,\rho_{ee}^{\rm ss}=\frac{\Gamma}{2}\cdot\frac{s}{1+s+(2\Delta/\Gamma)^2}$$
✅

This is exactly the formula implemented in scattering_rate.py's R_scatt() function and developed further in Section 08: verified symbolically end-to-end (from the raw steady-state $\rho_{ee}$ expression, through the $s$ substitution, to $\Gamma\rho_{ee}$) before writing it here, with zero residual difference from the code's formula at every step.

Section 02: rydberg.py

Quantum Defect Theory

Alkali atoms have one valence electron orbiting a closed-shell ionic core. Far from the core the electron sees a pure Coulomb $-e^2/r$ potential, just like hydrogen, but at small $r$ it penetrates the core and feels a stronger, non-Coulombic attraction. Quantum Defect Theory (QDT) captures this with a single parameter $\delta_l$ per orbital.

From Hydrogen to Alkali: the core-penetration correction

For hydrogen, the energy eigenvalues are exact:

Hydrogen energy levels
$$E_n^{\rm H} = -\frac{R_\infty}{n^2} = -\frac{13.6057\,{\rm eV}}{n^2}$$

where $R_\infty = m_e e^4 / (2\hbar^2) = 13.6057$ eV is the Rydberg energy. For an alkali atom the outer electron sees the same $1/r^2$ centrifugal barrier and the same asymptotic $-e/r$ Coulomb tail, but at short range it penetrates the $[{\rm core}]^+$ charge distribution. This shifts every energy level downward by an amount that depends on $l$ but is nearly independent of $n$ for large $n$. The corrected energy is:

Quantum defect formula: the core of rydberg.py
$$\boxed{E_{n,l} = -\frac{R_\infty}{(n - \delta_l)^2} \equiv -\frac{R_\infty}{n_{\rm eff}^2}}$$

The effective principal quantum number $n_{\rm eff} = n - \delta_l$ absorbs the entire core interaction into one number. The quantum defect $\delta_l$ is measured from NIST experimental energies and then used to extrapolate to high $n$ states never measured in the lab.

Why δ depends on l but not n

High-$l$ states ($l \geq 3$ for alkalis) have large centrifugal barriers: the classical turning point $r_{\rm min} \approx l(l+1)a_0/Z$ keeps the electron outside the core entirely. For these states $\delta_l \approx 0$ and the atom is perfectly hydrogenic. Low-$l$ states ($s, p, d$) have small $l$, low centrifugal barrier, and the electron dips deeply into the core on every orbit, hence large $\delta$.

Extracting δ_l from NIST data

The pipeline measures $\delta_l$ empirically. Given an experimental level at energy $E$ above the ground state, its binding energy relative to the ionization limit is $E_{\rm bind} = E_{\rm IE} - E$. Inverting the QDT formula:

Inverting QDT to extract n_eff (compute_quantum_defects in rydberg.py)
$$n_{\rm eff} = \sqrt{\frac{R_\infty}{E_{\rm bind}}} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \delta_l = n - n_{\rm eff}$$

This is done for every level in the NIST file and the results are averaged over all $n$ and all $J$ sublevels for each orbital type $l$:

Averaged quantum defect used in rydberg.py
$$\bar{\delta}_l = \frac{1}{N_l} \sum_{n,J} \left(n - \sqrt{\frac{R_\infty}{E_{\rm IE} - E_{n,l,J}}}\right)$$

Because $\delta_l(n)$ is very weakly $n$-dependent (Rydberg-Ritz correction $\delta_l \approx \delta_l^{(0)} + \delta_l^{(2)}/n_{\rm eff}^2 + \ldots$), averaging over all available $n$ gives an excellent single-number approximation for extrapolation to large $n$.

rydberg.py compute_quantum_defects(ionization_energy, levels) loops over the 4-tuples $(n, l, J, E)$ from parse_nist_file(), applies the formula above, and returns a dict {'s': δ_s, 'p': δ_p, 'd': δ_d, 'f': δ_f, 'g': δ_g}. The standard deviation printed alongside is a diagnostic: large std means the Rydberg-Ritz correction is needed or the NIST data contains multi-channel mixing.

Fine structure: spin-orbit splitting

The electron's spin magnetic moment interacts with the magnetic field it experiences moving through the electric field of the nucleus; this is spin-orbit coupling (SOC). The Hamiltonian term is:

Spin-orbit Hamiltonian
$$H_{\rm SOC} = \frac{\alpha^2}{2} \frac{Z_{\rm eff}}{r^3}\, \mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S}$$

For an alkali $p$ state this splits the $J = l \pm 1/2$ sublevels. The energy difference between $np_{3/2}$ (D2 line) and $np_{1/2}$ (D1 line) scales as:

Fine-structure splitting
$$\Delta E_{\rm SOC} = E(np_{3/2}) - E(np_{1/2}) \propto \frac{Z_{\rm eff}^4}{n_{\rm eff}^3}$$

This $Z^4$ scaling explains why Li has $\Delta E_{\rm SOC} = 0.043$ meV while Cs has $68.7$ meV and Fr has $209$ meV, a factor of $\sim 5000$ across the group. The pipeline reads both $J$ sublevels from NIST and stores them as separate states.

Rydberg Series & NIST Level Structure

The pipeline builds a complete level structure for the atom by combining J-resolved experimental energies from NIST (for low-$n$ states) with QDT-extrapolated energies for high-$n$ states. Understanding the NIST file format and the energy conventions is essential.

Energy reference convention

NIST Atomic Spectra Database reports energies in eV measured upward from the ground state (which has energy 0). The ionization limit (labelled "Limit" in the file) is the ionization energy $E_{\rm IE}$. The pipeline converts to ionization-relative energies:

Energy convention used throughout the pipeline
$$E_{\rm abs}(n,l,J) = E_{\rm NIST}(n,l,J) - E_{\rm IE} \qquad \text{(negative = bound)}$$

This convention places the ionization threshold at 0, bound states at negative energies, and the ground state at $-E_{\rm IE}$. It is the natural Rydberg convention and makes level diagrams immediately readable.

Optional alternate level energies only: arc_bridge.py exports ARC (Alkali Rydberg Calculator) energies into the same JSON schema under data_json/arc/<el>_rydberg.json. It never overwrites the NIST file, and it does not export oscillator strengths, Stark maps, pair interactions, or blockade radii. To try ARC energies in the downstream chain, copy that file over data_json/<el>_rydberg.json manually, then restore NIST afterward. Lifetimes still need NIST (or ORCA) $f$ values; Sr/Ca ARC levels are not a full alkali AMO path. See CLI and References.

J-resolved level storage

Each NIST row is a single $J$ sublevel. For a $p$ orbital there are two rows: $J=1/2$ and $J=3/2$. The pipeline stores them as separate entries with the tuple key $(n, l, J_{\rm str})$. The label encodes the $J$ value: $3p_{1/2}$, $3p_{3/2}$. For QDT-extrapolated states no $J$ is available so the label is plain ($10p$, $15d$).

Rydberg energy for QDT model levels (generate_rydberg_series)
$$E_{\rm QDT}(n, l) = -\frac{R_\infty}{(n - \bar{\delta}_l)^2}$$

The wavelength of a transition from the ground state at $E_{\rm gs} = -E_{\rm IE}$ to a Rydberg level at $E_{\rm abs}$ is:

Transition wavelength (used in all output JSON files)
$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E} = \frac{1239.841984\,{\rm eV\cdot nm}}{E_{\rm abs} - E_{\rm gs}}$$

Degeneracy and the deduplicate algorithm

The TD-DFT calculation (ORCA) does not enforce spin-orbit coupling by default. A $p$ level with $l=1$ produces three TD-DFT states ($m_l = -1, 0, +1$) at nearly identical energies, degenerate within the numerical noise of the SCF. The deduplicate function in runorca.py collapses these into a single physical level using gap-based grouping:

Gap-based degeneracy grouping (runorca.py)
$$\text{Group states } i, i+1 \text{ together if } E_{i+1} - E_i \leq \epsilon_{\rm tol} = 5\,{\rm meV}$$

Within each group, the representative state is chosen as the one with the highest oscillator strength $f$ (ties broken by $|c_{\rm dominant}|$). The field n_degenerate stores how many raw states were merged. This matters for computing total intensity: the physical oscillator strength of a degenerate level is $\sum_m f_m$, which equals $f_{\rm rep} \times n_{\rm deg}$ for symmetry-equivalent states.

What TD-DFT computes

Ground-state DFT gives the electronic density $\rho_0(\mathbf{r})$ and a set of Kohn-Sham (KS) orbitals $\{\phi_i\}$. TD-DFT describes the linear response of this density to a time-dependent electric perturbation. The Casida equation (in the Tamm-Dancoff approximation) is:

Tamm-Dancoff / TDA eigenvalue equation
$$\mathbf{A}\, \mathbf{X}_I = \Omega_I\, \mathbf{X}_I$$

where $\Omega_I$ is the $I$-th excitation energy, $\mathbf{X}_I$ is the transition amplitude vector, and the matrix elements are:

TDA matrix elements
$$A_{ia,jb} = (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i)\delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} + (ia|jb) - c_x(ij|ab) + (ia|f_{\rm xc}|jb)$$

Here $i,j$ are occupied and $a,b$ are virtual KS orbitals; $\varepsilon$ are KS eigenvalues; $(ia|jb)$ are Coulomb integrals; $c_x$ is the HF exchange fraction; and $f_{\rm xc}$ is the exchange-correlation kernel.

The pipeline uses CAM-B3LYP: a range-separated hybrid where $c_x$ varies from 0.19 at short range to 0.65 at long range (Coulomb attenuation). This is critical for Rydberg states whose diffuse character requires correct asymptotic exchange.

Oscillator strength from the transition dipole

ORCA computes the electric dipole transition moment between the ground state $|0\rangle$ and excited state $|I\rangle$:

Electric dipole transition moment and oscillator strength
$$\mathbf{d}_{0I} = \langle 0 | \hat{\mathbf{r}} | I \rangle = \sum_{ia} X_{ia}^I \langle \phi_i | \hat{\mathbf{r}} | \phi_a \rangle$$ $$f_{0I} = \frac{2m_e \Omega_I}{3\hbar e^2} |\mathbf{d}_{0I}|^2$$

The oscillator strength $f_{0I}$ is dimensionless and satisfies the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn (TRK) sum rule: $\sum_I f_{0I} = N_e$. In practice only the lowest $N_{\rm roots}$ states are computed (set by nroots in the ORCA input).

ORCA outputs the oscillator strengths in a separate "ABSORPTION SPECTRUM" table (not in the STATE blocks where the orbital coefficients appear). The parser parse_orca.py joins these two sections on the state index:

Section 05: transitions.py

Electric Dipole Selection Rules

Not every pair of energy levels can be connected by a photon. The electric dipole (E1) matrix element $\langle f | \hat{\mathbf{r}} | i \rangle$ must be non-zero. Group theory (the Wigner-Eckart theorem) tells us exactly when this happens.

Why the dipole matrix element vanishes

The dipole operator $\hat{r}$ transforms as a rank-1 spherical tensor under rotations: it has odd parity (changes sign under $\mathbf{r} \to -\mathbf{r}$). By the Wigner-Eckart theorem, the matrix element $\langle n', l', J', m_J' | \hat{r}_q | n, l, J, m_J \rangle$ vanishes unless:

E1 selection rules (enforced in transitions.py)
$$\Delta l = \pm 1 \qquad \text{(parity change; }l\text{ must change by 1)}$$ $$\Delta J = 0, \pm 1 \qquad \text{(triangular rule for rank-1 tensor coupling)}$$ $$J = 0 \not\to J = 0 \qquad \text{(forbidden by angular momentum conservation)}$$ $$\Delta m_J = 0, \pm 1 \qquad \text{(polarisation of absorbed/emitted photon)}$$

The $\Delta l = \pm 1$ rule (Laporte rule) is the strongest: it eliminates all $s \to s$, $p \to p$, $d \to d$ transitions. Combined with $\Delta J = 0, \pm 1$ it gives the familiar alkali D-line structure: $ns_{1/2} \to np_{1/2}$ (D1) and $ns_{1/2} \to np_{3/2}$ (D2).

Degeneracy counting for rate equations

The statistical weight (degeneracy) of a state with total angular momentum $J$ is:

Statistical weight
$$g_J = 2J + 1$$

Notation caution: in the Einstein master formula the symbols $g_l$ and $g_u$ mean the degeneracies of the lower and upper levels of that transition (usually $g_J=2J+1$). Separately, when the pipeline averages NIST gA over unresolved fine structure, it uses the orbital count:

Orbital degeneracy used in J-averaged NIST blocks
$$g_{\rm orb}(l) = 2l + 1 \quad (\text{number of }m_l\text{ sublevels})$$ $$g_{\rm upper}^{\rm orb} = 2l_{\rm upper} + 1, \qquad g_{\rm lower}^{\rm orb} = 2l_{\rm lower} + 1$$

That orbital weight (not $g_J$) enters the averaged $A\leftrightarrow f$ conversion for multiplet blocks. For a resolved line such as Na D2 ($3p_{3/2}\to 3s_{1/2}$) the code uses $g_u=4$, $g_l=2$. The ratio $g_{\rm lower}/g_{\rm upper}$ always normalises absorption $f$ to the emission rate for the degeneracies actually stored on that transition.

Einstein Coefficients & Radiative Lifetimes

Einstein's 1917 paper introduced three coefficients describing the interaction of a two-level system with a radiation field. The pipeline computes all three ($A_{21}$, $B_{21}$, $B_{12}$), plus the radiative lifetime, branching ratios, and saturation intensity for every state in the Rydberg series.

The three Einstein coefficients

Spontaneous emission: A₂₁

Rate of photon emission from $|2\rangle \to |1\rangle$ with no field present. Units: s⁻¹.

Physical origin: coupling to vacuum fluctuations of the EM field.

Stimulated processes: B₂₁, B₁₂

$B_{21}$: stimulated emission rate per unit spectral energy density $\rho(\nu)$.
$B_{12}$: absorption rate per $\rho(\nu)$. Units: m³ J⁻¹ s⁻².

The three coefficients are related by Einstein's equilibrium argument (detailed balance with a blackbody):

Einstein relations: fundamental connections between A and B
$$B_{21} = \frac{c^3}{8\pi h\nu^3}\, A_{21}$$ $$B_{12} = \frac{g_{\rm upper}}{g_{\rm lower}}\, B_{21}$$

The key formula: A₂₁ from oscillator strength

The oscillator strength $f_{lu}$ (absorption, lower $\to$ upper) is related to the Einstein $A$ coefficient by:

A coefficient from f (the master formula in lifetimes.py)
$$A_{ul} = \frac{e^2 \omega^2}{2\pi \varepsilon_0 m_e c^3} \cdot \frac{g_l}{g_u} \cdot f_{lu} = \frac{2\pi e^2}{\varepsilon_0 m_e c\,\lambda^2}\cdot\frac{g_l}{g_u}\cdot f_{lu} = \frac{6.6703 \times 10^{13}\,{\rm s^{-1}\,nm^2}}{\lambda^2[\rm nm]} \cdot \frac{g_l}{g_u} \cdot f_{lu}$$

The first two SI forms are identical once $\omega=2\pi c/\lambda$. The numerical prefactor $6.6703 \times 10^{13}$ s⁻¹ nm² is that coefficient with $\lambda$ in nanometres:

Derivation of EINSTEIN_PREFACTOR (constants.py)
$$P = \frac{2\pi e^2}{\varepsilon_0 m_e c} = \frac{2\pi\,(1.602176634\times10^{-19})^2}{8.8541878128\times10^{-12}\cdot 9.1093837015\times10^{-31}\cdot 2.99792458\times10^8}$$ $$\approx 6.6703\times10^{-5}\,{\rm m^{2}\,s^{-1}}$$ $${\rm PREFACTOR} = P\times(10^9\,{\rm nm/m})^2 = 6.6703\times10^{13}\,{\rm s^{-1}\,nm^2}$$

Radiative lifetime and branching ratios

The total spontaneous emission rate out of an excited state $|u\rangle$ is the sum of $A_{ul}$ over all allowed downward transitions:

Total decay rate and radiative lifetime
$$\Gamma = \sum_{l < u} A_{ul} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{\Gamma} = \frac{1}{\sum_l A_{ul}}$$

The branching ratio to a specific lower level $|l\rangle$ is:

$$\beta_{ul} = \frac{A_{ul}}{\Gamma}$$

For the Na $3p$ state there is only one decay channel ($3p \to 3s$), so $\beta = 1$. For higher states like $5p$, multiple channels contribute ($5p \to 3s$, $5p \to 4s$, $5p \to 3d$) and the branching ratios must sum to 1.

NIST gA averaging over J sublevels

NIST tabulates $gA = g_u \cdot A_{ul}$ for each individual $J_u \to J_l$ channel. To get an $f_{\rm eff}$ usable in the pipeline's orbital-averaged formula, the code in lifetimes.py computes a J-averaged $A$:

J-averaging of NIST gA values (lifetimes.py NIST loader)
$$A_{\rm avg} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{J_u} \sum_{J_l} gA(J_u \to J_l)}{\displaystyle\sum_{J_u} g_{J_u}} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\rm all channels} gA}{g_l^{\rm total}}$$ $$f_{\rm eff} = A_{\rm avg} \cdot \frac{\lambda^2}{\rm PREFACTOR} \cdot \frac{g_u}{g_l}$$

This converts the experimental $gA$ into the orbital-level $f_{\rm eff}$ that the main formula $A_{ul} = {\rm PREFACTOR}/\lambda^2 \cdot (g_l/g_u) \cdot f_{lu}$ expects. The uncertainty is propagated via quadrature: $\sigma_A = \sqrt{\sum_{\rm channels}(gA \cdot \delta_{\rm Acc})^2}$.

Section 07: spectra.py, lifetimes.py

Spectral Broadening Mechanisms

Real spectral lines are not delta functions. Three broadening mechanisms are modelled in the pipeline: natural (Lorentzian), Doppler (Gaussian), and instrumental (Gaussian). They combine into a Voigt profile.

Natural linewidth: Lorentzian profile

Spontaneous emission causes the excited state amplitude to decay as $e^{-\Gamma t/2}$ (where $\Gamma = 1/\tau$). By the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, the power spectral density of this decaying oscillator is a Lorentzian:

Natural lineshape and FWHM
$$S(\nu) \propto \frac{\Gamma_{\rm nat}/2\pi}{(\nu - \nu_0)^2 + (\Gamma_{\rm nat}/2)^2}$$ $$\Delta\nu_{\rm nat} = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi\tau} \qquad [\text{Hz, FWHM}]$$ $$\Delta E_{\rm nat} = \frac{\hbar}{\tau} \qquad [\text{eV, uncertainty principle}]$$

For the Na D2 line: $\tau = 16.236$ ns (pipeline), so $\Delta\nu_{\rm nat} = 9.80$ MHz, $\Delta E = 4.05 \times 10^{-8}$ eV. This is the fundamental quantum limit on linewidth set by spontaneous emission.

Doppler broadening: Gaussian profile

Atoms in a thermal gas have a Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution. An atom moving with velocity $v_z$ along the beam axis sees the laser frequency Doppler-shifted: $\nu' = \nu_0(1 + v_z/c)$. The distribution of $v_z$ is Gaussian, so the absorption profile is also Gaussian:

Doppler FWHM (used in lifetimes.py and compare_elements.py)
$$\Delta\nu_D = \frac{\nu_0}{c}\sqrt{\frac{8k_B T \ln 2}{m}}$$

where $m$ is the atomic mass in kg. In practice this is evaluated as:

Implementation in doppler_fwhm_Hz()
$$\Delta\nu_D = \frac{c}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{8 k_B[\text{eV/K}] \cdot T \cdot \ln 2}{mc^2[\text{eV}]}}$$ $$mc^2[\text{eV}] = m_{\rm amu} \times (931.494\,{\rm MeV})$$

At $T=300$ K, the Doppler FWHM for Na at 589 nm is $\sim1.32$ GHz, about $134\times$ the natural linewidth ($\Delta\nu_{\rm nat}\approx9.80$ MHz). This is the dominant broadening in a room-temperature vapour cell. In a cold atomic beam or MOT it is much smaller.

Peak absorption cross-section

The peak cross-section at line centre depends on which broadening mechanism dominates and on how the lineshape peak is defined. Start from the integrated cross-section (independent of lineshape):

Frequency-integrated absorption cross-section
$$\int\sigma(\nu)\,d\nu = \frac{\lambda^2 A_{21}}{8\pi}\frac{g_u}{g_l}$$

For a unit-normalised Lorentzian of FWHM $\Delta\nu_{\rm nat}=A/(2\pi)$, the peak height is $\phi_L(0)=2/(\pi\Delta\nu_{\rm nat})$, so

Literature Lorentzian peak (Steck isotropic / $m_J$-averaged)
$$\sigma_{\rm Lor}^{\rm peak} = \left(\int\sigma\,d\nu\right)\phi_L(0) = \frac{\lambda^2}{2\pi}\frac{g_u}{g_l}$$

That Lorentzian peak is what Steck's isotropic Na D2 sheet uses ($\sigma\sim1.10\times10^{-9}$ cm², $I_{\rm sat}\sim9.4$ mW/cm²). A closed cycling transition has a still larger peak cross-section $\sigma_0=3\lambda^2/(2\pi)$ (hence a lower $I_{\rm sat}$): $I_{\rm sat}=\pi hc\Gamma/(3\lambda^3)\approx6.26$ mW/cm² for Na D2.

The pipeline stores a different but internally consistent convention: replace the Lorentzian peak factor $2/(\pi\Delta\nu)$ by the rectangular estimate $1/\Delta\nu$ (area divided by FWHM). With $\Delta\nu_{\rm nat}=A/(2\pi)$ this collapses to the CompTable / lifetimes.py formula

Pipeline natural peak (JSON / CompTable)
$$\sigma_{\rm nat}^{\rm peak} = \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi}\cdot\frac{g_u}{g_l}\cdot\frac{A}{\Delta\nu_{\rm nat}} = \frac{\lambda^2}{4}\frac{g_u}{g_l}$$

For Na D2 ($g_u/g_l=2$) this gives $\sigma_{\rm nat}\approx1.736\times10^{-9}$ cm² and $I_{\rm sat}^{\rm nat}\approx5.98$ mW/cm² - the numbers in Na_lifetimes.json and comparison_table.json. Keep the Lorentzian / cycling values in mind when comparing to Steck; do not mix conventions inside one calculation.

Pipeline Doppler peak (CompTable scaling)
$$\sigma_{\rm Dop}^{\rm peak} = \sigma_{\rm nat}^{\rm peak}\cdot\frac{\Delta\nu_{\rm nat}}{\Delta\nu_D}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\ln 2}{\pi}}$$

This is what compare_elements.py and lifetimes.py write for Doppler columns. A unit-normalised Gaussian of FWHM $\Delta\nu_D$ has peak $\phi_G(0)=2\sqrt{\ln 2/\pi}/\Delta\nu_D$, so the proper Gaussian peak built from $\int\sigma\,d\nu$ is larger by a factor of 2 than the CompTable scaling above. The pipeline keeps the CompTable form so every table and JSON agree.

At $T=300$ K for Na D2, $\Delta\nu_D/\Delta\nu_{\rm nat}\approx134$, so $\sigma_{\rm Dop}$ is orders of magnitude below $\sigma_{\rm nat}$: the absorption is spread over a much wider frequency range.

The factor $\sqrt{\ln 2 / \pi}$ in the CompTable Doppler formula is the Gaussian shape factor that maps natural peak $\times$ linewidth ratio onto the Doppler-averaged peak used in the HTML slider. It is not the full $\phi_G(0)$ normalisation (that would carry an extra 2). Natural and Doppler $I_{\rm sat}$ both use $I_{\rm sat}=h\nu/(2\sigma_{\rm peak}\tau)$ with the matching $\sigma$.

Section 08: scattering_rate.py, compare_elements.py

Laser Cooling Physics

Each absorbed photon transfers momentum $\hbar k$ to the atom. At high enough scattering rates this radiation pressure force can decelerate and cool atoms. The pipeline computes all key laser cooling parameters.

The photon scattering rate

For a two-level atom driven by a laser of intensity $I$ and detuning $\Delta = \omega - \omega_0$, the steady-state excited state population and hence photon scattering rate is:

Photon scattering rate: the Optical Bloch Equation result
$$R_{\rm scatt}(I,\Delta) = \frac{\Gamma}{2} \cdot \frac{s}{1 + s + (2\Delta/\Gamma)^2}$$ $$s = \frac{I}{I_{\rm sat}} \quad \text{(saturation parameter)}$$

At $\Delta = 0$ and $I \to \infty$: $R_{\rm scatt} \to \Gamma/2 = 1/(2\tau)$. This is the maximum physical scattering rate: the atom spends equal time in ground and excited states. At $I = I_{\rm sat}$ and $\Delta = 0$: $R_{\rm scatt} = \Gamma/4$.

Saturation intensity

The saturation intensity $I_{\rm sat}$ is defined as the intensity at which the on-resonance ($\Delta=0$) scattering rate is half its maximum: $R_{\rm scatt}(I_{\rm sat}, 0) = \Gamma/4$. From the above formula:

Saturation intensity (pipeline definition)
$$I_{\rm sat} = \frac{h\nu}{2\sigma_{\rm peak}\,\tau}$$

This is what the pipeline computes: isat_from_sigma(wl_nm, sigma_m2, tau_s) in compare_elements.py / lifetimes.py, using the pipeline $\sigma_{\rm peak}$ of Section 07. $I_{\rm sat}$ is quoted separately for natural and Doppler broadening because $\sigma_{\rm peak}$ depends on the dominant broadening mechanism. For Doppler broadening $\sigma_{\rm peak}$ is smaller, so $I_{\rm sat}$ is larger; more intensity is required to saturate the atom.

The closed-cycling formula often seen in MOT notes, $I_{\rm sat}=\pi hc\Gamma/(3\lambda^3)$, is not equivalent to the pipeline expression. It belongs with $\sigma_0=3\lambda^2/(2\pi)$ (stretched $m_F$ cycling). For isotropic / $m_J$-averaged light the Lorentzian peak $\sigma=\lambda^2/(2\pi)\,(g_u/g_l)$ gives a higher $I_{\rm sat}$ ($\sim9.4$ mW/cm² for Na D2); the pipeline $\lambda^2/4$ convention gives $\sim5.98$ mW/cm². Always state which $\sigma$ you used.

Radiation pressure force and Doppler cooling

The radiation pressure force from a single beam on a moving atom is:

Radiation pressure force (scattering_rate.py)
$$F_{\rm rad} = \hbar k \cdot R_{\rm scatt}(I, \Delta)$$

For two counter-propagating beams with detuning $\Delta < 0$ (red-detuned), an atom moving with velocity $v$ sees the forward beam blue-shifted ($\Delta_+ = \Delta - kv$) and the backward beam red-shifted ($\Delta_- = \Delta + kv$). The net force is:

Doppler cooling net force
$$F_{\rm net} = \hbar k \Big[R_{\rm scatt}(I, \Delta - kv) - R_{\rm scatt}(I, \Delta + kv)\Big]$$

Near $v = 0$ and at optimal detuning $\Delta = -\Gamma/2$, this is a viscous damping force $F \approx -\alpha v$ with friction coefficient:

$$\alpha = -\hbar k^2 \cdot \frac{8s|\Delta|/\Gamma}{(1 + s + (2\Delta/\Gamma)^2)^2}$$

Recoil, Doppler limit and capture velocity

The minimum temperature achievable by Doppler cooling (set by the balance between cooling by friction and heating by photon recoil) is:

Doppler cooling limit (compare_elements.py)
$$T_{\rm Dop} = \frac{\hbar\Gamma}{2k_B} \qquad \text{(minimum Doppler cooling temperature)}$$
Recoil velocity and energy
$$v_{\rm rec} = \frac{\hbar k}{m} = \frac{h}{m\lambda} \qquad [\rm m/s]$$ $$E_{\rm rec} = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m} = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\rm rec}^2 \qquad \Leftrightarrow \qquad T_{\rm rec} = \frac{E_{\rm rec}}{k_B}\quad[\rm nK]$$
Capture velocity: maximum velocity that can be cooled
$$v_{\rm cap} = \frac{\Gamma}{k} = \Gamma\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}$$

For Na D2 from the pipeline JSON / CompTable: $T_{\rm Dop} = 235\,\mu\rm K$, $v_{\rm rec} = 2.946\,\rm cm/s$, $E_{\rm rec}/k_B = 1200\,\rm nK$, $v_{\rm cap} = 5.78\,\rm m/s$. Steck's sheet quotes nearly the same Doppler / recoil scales; $I_{\rm sat}$ differs by convention (see above and Section 07).

Oscillator Strengths: Numerov primary, Coulomb last resort

For transitions not covered by NIST, literature, or a valid ORCA energy match (mainly high-$n$ Rydberg channels), the pipeline uses QD Numerov radial dipoles (qdt_radial.py), scaled by a per-element NIST-overlap median. The Bates-Damgaard Coulomb closed form remains only as a last resort if Numerov returns 0.

The oscillator strength defined

The dimensionless absorption oscillator strength $f_{lu}$ is the ratio of the actual transition probability to that of a classical harmonic oscillator at the same frequency. It is directly related to the electric dipole transition matrix element:

Absorption oscillator strength
$$f_{lu} = \frac{2m_e\omega_{lu}}{3\hbar}\cdot\frac{g_u}{g_l}\cdot|\langle l|\hat{r}|u\rangle|^2$$

It satisfies the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rule $\sum_u f_{lu} = N_e$ (number of electrons), which is used as a consistency check in ab initio codes.

The Coulomb approximation (Bates & Damgaard 1949) - last resort

For Rydberg states the radial wavefunction in the outer region is well approximated by Coulomb functions with $n \to n^* = n - \delta_l$. The pipeline prefers the QD Numerov radial integral for the same $n^*$ inputs; the closed-form Coulomb estimate below is used only when Numerov fails. Implementation: coulomb_fosc in lifetimes.py / alpha_core.py.

Coulomb approximation radial matrix element
$$|\langle n^*_l\, l\,|\, r\,|\, n^*_u\, l'\rangle|^2 \approx \frac{(n^*_u n^*_l)^3}{(n_u^{*2} - n_l^{*2})^3}$$
Full Coulomb oscillator strength formula (coulomb_fosc in lifetimes.py)
$$f_{lu} = \frac{4}{3}\,\Delta E_{\rm Ry}\cdot C_{\rm ang}\cdot\frac{(n^*_u n^*_l)^3}{(n_u^{*2} - n_l^{*2})^3}\cdot\frac{g_u}{g_l}$$ $$C_{\rm ang} = \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{l_{\rm max}}{2l_l + 1}, \qquad l_{\rm max} = \max(l_u, l_l)$$ $$\Delta E_{\rm Ry} = \frac{1}{n_l^{*2}} - \frac{1}{n_u^{*2}} \qquad \text{(in Rydberg units)}$$

The formula diverges when $n_u^{*2} \approx n_l^{*2}$ (same-$n$ transitions), which the code guards against explicitly:

# lifetimes.py: guard against near-degenerate n_eff if (n_eff_u**2 - n_eff_l**2) < 0.5 * n_eff_u: return 0.0 # Coulomb approximation invalid for same-n

The reliability thresholds: $n^* > 4$ gives ~30% accuracy; $n^* > 2.5$ with non-ground lower state gives ~50% accuracy; below this the approximation is not used (returns $f=0$, labelled 'unknown').

Connecting f, A, B, σ, I_sat: the complete chain

Every observable in the pipeline traces back to a single $f_{lu}$ value. Here is the complete chain of derived quantities:

Complete derivation chain
$$f_{lu} \xrightarrow{\times\,{\rm PREF}/\lambda^2 \cdot g_l/g_u} A_{ul}\,[{\rm s}^{-1}] \xrightarrow{1/\sum A} \tau\,[{\rm ns}]$$ $$A_{ul} \xrightarrow{\times\,c^3/(8\pi h\nu^3)} B_{21}\,[{\rm m^3\,J^{-1}\,s^{-2}}]$$ $$A_{ul} \xrightarrow{(\lambda^2/8\pi)(g_u/g_l)(A/\Delta\nu_{\rm nat})} \sigma_{\rm nat}^{\rm peak}=\tfrac{\lambda^2}{4}\tfrac{g_u}{g_l}\,[{\rm cm}^2]$$ $$\sigma_{\rm nat}^{\rm peak} \xrightarrow{h\nu/(2\sigma\tau)} I_{\rm sat}\,[{\rm mW/cm}^2]$$ $$I_{\rm sat} \xrightarrow{{\rm two\text{-}level\,OBE}} R_{\rm scatt}(I,\Delta)\,[{\rm s}^{-1}] \xrightarrow{\times\,\hbar k} F_{\rm rad}\,[{\rm aN}]$$

Uncertainty propagation

Every quantity carries a fractional uncertainty $\delta$ inherited from the source of $f_{lu}$. Error propagation through the chain:

Error propagation through the lifetime calculation
$$\sigma_A = A_{ul} \cdot \delta_f \qquad \text{(linear in }f\text{)}$$ $$\sigma_\Gamma = \sqrt{\sum_l \sigma_{A_{ul}}^2} \qquad \text{(quadrature sum)}$$ $$\sigma_\tau = \frac{\sigma_\Gamma}{\Gamma^2} \qquad \text{(propagation: }\tau = 1/\Gamma\text{)}$$

The quality grade shown in the lifetime table is the NIST accuracy code (AAA/AA/A/B/C/D/E) of the dominant decay channel, the channel contributing more than 5% of the total $A$.

This section records the remaining formulas the trap branch actually evaluates: imaginary-frequency polarizability, Casimir-Polder $C_6$, ionic-core correction, and the conservative ODT quantities already previewed in §01 G.

Real-frequency sum-over-states (code form)

With oscillator strengths $f_k$ and signed energy gaps $\omega_k$ (atomic units), alpha_real() implements

Atomic-unit form: exactly alpha_real() in alpha_core.py
$$\alpha(\omega)=\sum_k \mathrm{sign}_k\,\frac{f_k}{\omega_k^2-\omega^2}$$

$\mathrm{sign}_k=+1$ for upward couplings from the state of interest and $-1$ for downward ones. Near poles a guard band returns NaN so resonance spikes do not pollute plots.

Imaginary-frequency polarizability and $C_6$

The van der Waals coefficient between two identical ground-state atoms is the Casimir-Polder integral over polarizability at imaginary frequency (atomic units):

Imaginary-frequency polarizability (alpha_imag_freq)
$$\alpha(i\xi)=\sum_k\frac{f_k}{\omega_k^2+\xi^2}$$
Casimir-Polder $C_6$
$$C_6=\frac{3}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\big[\alpha(i\xi)\big]^2\,d\xi$$

polarizability.py evaluates the integral by Gauss-Legendre quadrature on a log-mapped grid. Heteronuclear $C_6^{AB}$ uses the standard combining rule when comparing species.

Ionic core polarizability

A pure valence sum-over-states misses the closed-shell ionic core. The pipeline adds a Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn-constrained one-pole Padé term with tabulated $\alpha_{\rm core}$ (Mitroy / Safronova / Schwerdtfeger compilations) so that $\alpha(0)$ is corrected by $0.1\%$ (Li) to $\sim6\%$ (Fr):

Core one-pole (ALPHA_CORE_AU in alpha_core.py)
$$\omega_c=\sqrt{\frac{N_{\rm core}}{\alpha_c}},\qquad \alpha_{\rm core}(\omega)=\frac{N_{\rm core}}{\omega_c^2-\omega^2}$$
!

Ion polarizability is unsupported. polarizability.py, blackbody, and tweezer currently run on neutral alkalis only. For a closed-shell cation such as Na+, summing bound transitions alone recovers only a fraction of the literature $\alpha(0)\approx1.00$ au (Lim et al.), because most oscillator strength lies in the continuum. Use the ion path for levels and spectra (CLI: ions). The core correction above is the Na+-like contribution inside neutral Na, not a standalone Na+ polarizability calculation.

Magic wavelengths and trap depth

Magic wavelengths solve $\alpha_{\rm gs}(\omega)=\alpha_{\rm ex}(\omega)$. Trap depth and Gaussian-tweezer frequencies are the Grimm formulas of §01 G (U_joules, trap_frequencies_Hz). Outputs: data_json/<el>_polarizability.json and plots/<el>/<el>_polarizability.html (full-viewport; intensity / point calculator panel). For Na the pipeline reports $\alpha(0)=162.61$ au vs expt $162.7$, and $C_6\approx1558$ au vs expt $1556$ (see Na Step 7).

Section 11: blackbody.py

Blackbody Radiation Shifts and Depopulation

A thermal photon bath acts as a slowly varying AC field. For alkali-like atoms at laboratory temperatures the leading energy shift is the static polarizability term; a smaller dynamic correction integrates $\alpha(\omega)$ against the Planck spectrum. Separately, the same bath drives bound-bound depopulation rates $\Gamma_{\rm BBR}$ on the E1 network (continuum photoionization via Beterov closed form; see below).

Static (Itano) term

Itano thermal field (Itano, Lewis & Wineland, PRA 25, 1233 (1982))
$$\langle E^2\rangle_T=(831.9\,\mathrm{V/m})^2\left(\frac{T}{300\,\mathrm{K}}\right)^4$$
Static BBR energy / frequency shift
$$\Delta E(T)=-\tfrac12\,\alpha(0)\,\langle E^2\rangle_T \quad\text{(a.u.)},\qquad \Delta\nu=\Delta E\cdot(E_h/h)$$

Differential clock / transition shifts are $\Delta\nu_{\rm diff}=\Delta\nu_{\rm ex}-\Delta\nu_{\rm gs}$.

Dynamic correction (Planck integral)

Allowing for frequency dependence of $\alpha$ across the thermal spectrum (Beloy et al., arXiv:2507.00948, Sec. II):

Dynamic BBR shift
$$\Delta E(T)=-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\infty u(\omega,T)\,\alpha_{\rm SI}(\omega)\,d\omega$$ $$u(\omega,T)=\frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1}$$

Validated in-code against (1) the Itano $\langle E^2\rangle_T$ limit to $<0.02\%$ and (2) the Farley-Wing high-$n$ closed form at 300 K. For ground-state alkalis the dynamic correction is only a few tenths of a percent of the static term (unlike Sr clocks, where it can reach $\sim7\%$). For Na at 300 K: $\Delta\nu_{\rm gs}=-1.40$ Hz (static), $-1.40$ Hz (full dynamic, JSON $-1.404$); D1 differential $\approx-0.52$ Hz (Na Step 7). Outputs: data_json/<el>_blackbody.json and fullscreen HTML plots under plots/<el>/: a chooser hub <el>_blackbody.html plus separate _shifts, _rates, _pi, _alpha pages (dropdown switches the view).

Bound-bound depopulation rates

Besides energy shifts, the thermal bath drives stimulated emission and absorption on the E1 network from *_lifetimes.json. With $\bar n(\omega,T)=1/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1)$ and spontaneous $A_{ul}$:

Bound-bound BBR channel rates
$$\Gamma_{u\to l}^{\rm stim}=A_{ul}\,\bar n,\qquad \Gamma_{l\to u}^{\rm abs}=A_{ul}\,\frac{g_u}{g_l}\,\bar n$$ $$\Gamma_{\rm BBR}(s,T)=\sum_p\Gamma_{s\to p},\qquad \tau_{\rm eff}=\bigl(\Gamma_{\rm rad}+\Gamma_{\rm BBR}\bigr)^{-1}$$

Default table: states with $n\ge n_{\rm gs}+2$ (--n-min to override). Optical D-lines at 300 K give $\Gamma_{\rm BBR}/\Gamma_{\rm rad}\sim10^{-19}$ (sanity). High-$n$ $s$-states show a growing ratio (Na $10s\sim3.5\%$, $25s\sim25\%$ at 300 K). Continuum photoionization is handled separately (next subsection). CLI: python blackbody.py Na --T 300, --n-min 10, --no-rates.

Continuum photoionization / quenching

Direct BBR photoionization uses Beterov eq. (27) (NJP 11, 013052 (2009)) with element/$L$-dependent $A_L$ (Table 2) and quantum-defect phase weights (Table 1):

Beterov $\Gamma_{\rm PI}$ (s$^{-1}$, $T$ in K)
$$\Gamma_{\rm PI}=A_L\frac{11500\,T}{n^{*\,7/3}} \Bigl[\cos^{2}\!\bigl(\Delta_L^{+}+\tfrac{\pi}{6}\bigr) +\cos^{2}\!\bigl(\Delta_L^{-}-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\bigr)\Bigr] \ln\!\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-157890/(T n^{*2})}}\right)$$
$nS$: keep only the $L{\to}L{+}1$ term. $\Gamma_{\rm quench}=\Gamma_{\rm BBR}+\Gamma_{\rm PI}$ (free space).

Optional selective field ionization of BBR-populated $n'\ge n_c$ (eq. 30): --field E in V/cm with $E_c\approx3.2\times10^{8}\,n_{\rm eff}^{-4}$. SFI is not folded into free-space $\tau_{\rm eff}$.

Multi-step mixing (eq. 35): transfer to $\Delta L=\pm1$ neighbors (default $|\Delta n|\le3$) via $A+W_{\rm BBR}$, then those states ionize under a gated window $t_{\rm eff}=\tau(e^{-t_1/\tau}-e^{-t_2/\tau})$. Defaults match the Na experiment ($t_1=0.3$ µs, $t_2=2.1$ µs). Reported rates: $\Gamma_{\rm mix,PI}$, $\Gamma_{\rm mix,SFI}$, and $\Gamma_{\rm ion}=\Gamma_{\rm PI}+\Gamma_{\rm SFI}+\Gamma_{\rm mix}$. Free-space quench still uses $\Gamma_{\rm BBR}+\Gamma_{\rm PI}$ only. Na @ 300 K: $20s$ $\Gamma_{\rm PI}\sim216$/s; with --field 100, $\Gamma_{\rm SFI}\sim88$/s, $\Gamma_{\rm mix,PI}\sim5.7$/s, $\Gamma_{\rm ion}\sim313$/s. CLI: --pi-n-min, --no-pi, --field, --no-mix, --t1, --t2, --mix-dn-max.

High-$n$ Rydberg $\alpha(0)$ / static shifts

Optical BBR shifts use the cached ground / D-line networks from *_polarizability.json. For arbitrary Rydberg labels, blackbody.py builds an on-the-fly sum-over-states $\alpha(0)$ from *_transitions.json (NIST $f$ where available, Coulomb fallback otherwise) and reports the static Itano shift plus a damped dynamic Planck integral (Lorentzian $\Gamma=A_{\rm total}$ from lifetimes; IR resonances near $k_BT/\hbar$). Default $n$ window: $n_{\rm gs}+2$ to $n_{\max}-n_{\rm pad}$ ($n_{\rm pad}=5$). All alkalis use series $n_{\max}=50$ (alpha window through $n=45$).

Static high-$n$ BBR (Itano)
$$\alpha(0)=\sum_k\mathrm{sign}_k\,\frac{f_k}{\Delta E_k^2},\qquad \Delta\nu=-\tfrac12\alpha(0)\,\langle E^2\rangle_T\,(E_h/h)$$
Farley-Wing high-$n$ asymptote + damped dyn
$$\Delta E_{\rm FW}=\frac{\pi(k_BT)^2}{3c^3} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta\nu_{\rm FW}=+2416.666\,\mathrm{Hz}\ (T=300\,\mathrm{K})$$ $$\alpha(\omega)=\sum_k\mathrm{sign}_k\, \frac{f_k(\omega_k^2-\omega^2)} {(\omega_k^2-\omega^2)^2+(\Gamma_k\omega)^2}$$

Static Itano grows as $\alpha(0)\sim n^7$ and is not physical once IR partners sit in the thermal band; use $\Delta\nu_{\rm dyn}$. The dyn network applies TRK closure (scale signed $f$ to 1), a QDT discrete tail beyond series $n_{\max}$, and Kramers continuum bins for any residual strength - without this, Coulomb same-$n$ $f$ overshoots TRK and dyn sits ~3× above Farley–Wing. Na @ 300 K: $20s$ $\Delta\nu_{\rm dyn}\sim+2630$ Hz (FW ratio $\sim1.09$); $40s\sim+2320$ Hz ($\sim0.96$). CLI: --shift-n-min / --shift-n-max, --state 20s,25s, --no-rydberg-shifts, --no-rydberg-dyn, --no-rydberg-cont, --n-tail-max; debug: python polarizability.py Na --state 20s.

Section 12: tweezer.py

Optical-Tweezer Scattering and Heating

Conservative trap depth and frequencies live in polarizability.py. What remained was the dissipative channel: how often the atom scatters a trap photon, how fast that heats the motion, and roughly how long until recoil heating empties the tweezer. This is not the near-resonant cooling theory of Section 08 (scattering_rate.py).

Complex polarizability with finite linewidths

Attach $\Gamma_k=A_{\rm total}$ of each upper state from lifetimes.py to the cached transitions_gs network:

Complex dynamic polarizability (tweezer.alpha_complex)
$$\alpha(\omega)=\sum_k \mathrm{sign}_k\,\frac{f_k}{\omega_k^2-\omega^2-i\Gamma_k\omega}$$

Ionic-core channels (source=='core') are dropped from $\mathrm{Im}[\alpha]$: they do not represent optical-frequency scattering in this model.

Photon scattering from $\mathrm{Im}[\alpha]$

Grimm scattering rate from Im[α]
$$R_{\rm sc}=\frac{4\pi a_0^3}{\hbar c}\,\mathrm{Im}[\alpha(\omega)]\,I$$

Far off a single resonance this recovers the two-level Grimm check $R_{\rm sc}\approx(\Gamma/|\Delta|)|U|/\hbar$. At typical tweezer wavelengths ($|\Delta|$ optical-scale) the full multi-level $\mathrm{Im}[\alpha]$ sum is required; the RWA two-level ratio is only $\sim0.7$ at 1064 nm for Na D2.

Recoil heating and trap lifetime

Recoil energy, heating rate, trap-heating lifetime
$$E_r=\frac{h^2}{2m\lambda^2},\qquad \dot E=2 E_r\,R_{\rm sc},\qquad \tau_{\rm heat}\approx\frac{|U|}{\dot E}$$

The factor of two counts absorption of a trap photon plus isotropic spontaneous emission. $\tau_{\rm heat}$ is an order-of-magnitude estimate (deposit one trap depth of energy); real hold times also depend on background gas, intensity noise, and tunneling. Outputs: data_json/<el>_tweezer.json and plots/<el>/<el>_tweezer.html ($R_{\rm sc}$, heating, and $\tau_{\rm heat}$ vs $\lambda$). HTML fills the viewport (same fullscreen CSS pattern as polarizability / Feshbach) and includes a live point calculator (λ, $I$, $w_0$).

✅

Na @ 1064 nm, 50 kW/cm², $w_0=1$ µm: $U/k_B\approx-26$ µK, $R_{\rm sc}\approx0.105$ s⁻¹, heating $\approx0.077$ µK/s, $\tau_{\rm heat}\sim340$ s, dominated by the D2 then D1 virtual channels.

Section 13: hyperfine.py

Hyperfine Structure and Zeeman / Breit-Rabi

Nuclear spin $\mathbf I$ couples to electronic $\mathbf J$ to form $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf J$. hyperfine.py builds zero-field $F$ manifolds from magnetic-dipole ($A$) and electric-quadrupole ($B$) constants, then diagonalizes the Breit-Rabi Hamiltonian vs $B$.

Zero-field intervals

Magnetic dipole + quadrupole (Casimir)
$$E_F=\tfrac12 A\,K + B\,\frac{\tfrac34 K(K+1)-I(I+1)J(J+1)}{2I(2I-1)J(2J-1)}$$ $$K=F(F+1)-I(I+1)-J(J+1)$$

Landé $g_J$ defaults from the fine-structure formula; $g_I$ and nuclear moments come from nuclear_data.json (rebuildable via --build-nuclear-data). For $^{23}$Na ground state: $I=3/2$, $A=885.813$ MHz, clock $F=2\leftrightarrow F=1$ at $1771.626$ MHz. For $^{133}$Cs: $I=7/2$, $A\approx2298.158$ MHz, clock $F=4\leftrightarrow F=3$ at $9192.632$ MHz (the SI second definition).

Ground-state microwave clock frequency

When the ground multiplet has at least two $F$ levels, the pipeline reports the zero-field $\Delta F=1$ interval $F_{\rm max}\leftrightarrow F_{\rm max}-1$ as special.microwave_clock in <el>_hyperfine.json: frequency in MHz / GHz / Hz and free-space wavelength. The $m_F=0$ component is first-order $B$-insensitive (the usual clock / fountain transition). The hyperfine HTML annotates that interval on the ground column, highlights it in the splittings bar chart, and exposes a Clock frequency panel (dropdown) with the numeric card. Full Zeeman / Breit-Rabi curves stay in <el>_zeeman.html.

Breit-Rabi Zeeman diagram

At finite $B$ the good quantum numbers are $m_F$ (weak field) then $m_I,m_J$ (Paschen-Back). The code diagonalizes $H_{\rm hf}+H_Z$ on a $B$-grid (default $0\to500$ G) and writes interactive HTML. Isotope selection: --isotope A (defaults to the most abundant stable isotope; Li defaults to $^7$Li for hyperfine).

Section 14: feshbach.py

Magnetic Feshbach Resonances

Near a closed-channel bound state that crosses the open-channel threshold, the $s$-wave scattering length diverges. The pipeline does not solve coupled channels; it evaluates the standard isolated-resonance product form using literature $(B_0,\Delta,a_{\rm bg})$ tables you maintain in data_json/<el>_feshbach.json.

Scattering length and elastic cross section

Chin / Moerdijk isolated resonance
$$a(B)=a_{\rm bg}\prod_i\Bigl(1-\frac{\Delta_i}{B-B_{0,i}}\Bigr)$$
Unitary-limited elastic cross section
$$\sigma_{\rm el}=\frac{4\pi a^2}{1+k^2 a^2},\qquad k=\frac{\sqrt{2\mu k_B T}}{\hbar}$$

Mean scattering length $\bar a$ and van der Waals length $\beta_6$ are reported from $C_6$ (polarizability JSON or C6_EXPT). Coverage: Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs. Fr skipped: no published experimental magnetic FR parameters for trapped Fr.

Reads Na_rydberg.json. Injects the ground state from the ground_state block: $3s$, $l=s$, $J=1/2$, $E=-5.1391$ eV. Then loops over all pairs $(i,j)$ with $E_i < E_j$ and applies:

Both conditions must be true for a transition to be kept
$$|l_j - l_i| = 1 \quad\text{AND}\quad |J_j - J_i| \leq 1 \quad\text{AND}\quad \neg(J_i=0\to J_j=0)$$

Step 4: lifetimes.py: Einstein A, τ, and all derived quantities

Reads all three JSON files. Ground state label = '3s' (from ground_state.label). Loops over every transition and assigns an oscillator strength source by priority.

Priority 1: NIST f-values from f_values/Na_lines.txt

The NIST lines file contains one row per $J_u \to J_l$ channel with columns obs_wl_air(nm), Ei(eV), Ek(eV), gA(s⁻¹), conf_i, conf_k, J_i, J_k, Acc. For the Na D2 line there is exactly one row: $3p_{3/2}$ ($J=3/2$) $\to$ $3s$ ($J=1/2$). The $3s$ ground state has only one sublevel ($J=1/2$), so there is no second lower channel.

NIST f / A for the resolved D2 line (pipeline JSON)
$$f_{\rm D2} = 0.641\quad(\text{Acc: AA},\;\delta_f\simeq1\%)$$ $$A_{3p_{3/2}\to 3s} = \frac{6.6703\times10^{13}}{(589.1575\,{\rm nm})^2}\cdot\frac{g_l}{g_u}\cdot f_{\rm D2} = 6.159\times10^7\,{\rm s}^{-1}$$ $$gA_{\rm D2} = g_u A = 4\times 6.159\times10^7 = 2.464\times10^8\,{\rm s}^{-1}$$

For this closed $J$-resolved line there is a single lower channel, so no multiplet $gA$ average is required. Multiplet averaging ($A_{\rm avg}=\sum gA/g_l^{\rm tot}$, then $f_{\rm eff}$ from $A_{\rm avg}$) still applies to unresolved NIST blocks elsewhere in the loader.

Lifetime from $A$

Lifetime of Na 3p₃/₂ (D2 upper level)
$$\Gamma = \sum_l A_{ul} = A_{3p3/2\to3s} = 6.159\times10^7\,{\rm s}^{-1}$$ $$\tau = \frac{1}{\Gamma} = 16.236\,{\rm ns} \quad (\text{Steck / accepted }\sim16.23\,{\rm ns})$$ $$\sigma_\tau = \frac{\sigma_\Gamma}{\Gamma^2} = \frac{A \cdot \delta_f}{\Gamma^2} \approx 0.16\,{\rm ns}\quad(\text{from Acc AA})$$

Priority 4: QD Numerov for high-n Rydberg (e.g. 20s → 4p)

Channels such as $20s\to 4p$ have no NIST/literature $f$, and ORCA does not assign a root under the $0.10\,\mathrm{eV}$ / $\mathrm{IE}-0.25\,\mathrm{eV}$ match window. The pipeline uses QD Numerov radial $f$, then multiplies by the Na NIST-overlap scale $s=\mathrm{median}(f_{\rm NIST}/f_{\rm Numerov})=1.520$ (129 inliers). Bare multiplet $f$ for $20s\to 4p$:

Scaled Numerov f for Na 20s → 4p
$$n^*_{20s} = 20 - \delta_s = 20 - 1.3505 = 18.649,\quad n^*_{4p} = 4 - \delta_p = 4 - 0.8650 = 3.135$$ $$f_{\rm bare} = f_{\rm Numerov}(20s,4p) = 8.289\times 10^{-5}$$ $$f = s\cdot f_{\rm bare} = 1.520\times 8.289\times 10^{-5} = 1.260\times 10^{-4}$$

Einstein $A$ for the $20s\to 4p_{3/2}$ channel ($\lambda=920.69\,\mathrm{nm}$, $g_l/g_u=3$):

Na 20s → 4p₃/₂ via scaled Numerov
$$A = \frac{6.6703\times 10^{13}}{(920.69)^2}\cdot\frac{3}{1}\cdot 1.260\times 10^{-4} \approx 2.97\times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$$ $$\text{source tag: Numerov (not Coulomb)}$$

The $20s$ lifetime itself is still set mostly by NIST $20s\to 3p$ channels; Numerov fills the many mid-/high-$n$ partners that NIST does not tabulate. Coulomb is not used for this channel.

Einstein B for Na D2

Peak absorption cross-sections (pipeline convention)

Na D2 I_sat (both broadening regimes)

Step 6: compare_elements.py & scattering_rate.py: laser cooling parameters

Photon recoil

Na D2 recoil (compare_elements.py)
$$v_{\rm rec} = \frac{\hbar k}{m} = \frac{h}{m\lambda} = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{22.990\times1.661\times10^{-27}\times589.158\times10^{-9}} = 0.02946\,{\rm m/s} = 2.946\,{\rm cm/s}$$ $$E_{\rm rec} = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\rm rec}^2 = \frac{1}{2}\times3.818\times10^{-26}\times(0.02946)^2 = 1.655\times10^{-29}\,{\rm J} = 1200\,{\rm nK}$$

Doppler cooling limit

Na Doppler cooling temperature
$$T_{\rm Dop} = \frac{\hbar\Gamma}{2k_B} = \frac{6.582\times10^{-16}\times6.159\times10^7}{2\times8.617\times10^{-5}} = 235\,\mu{\rm K}$$

Photon scattering rate and radiation pressure

Na D2 scattering rate (scattering_rate.py) at Δ=-Γ/2, I=I_sat

Section 18: compare_elements.py

Reading the Alkali Comparison Table

How to read plots/comparison_table.html: the interactive Li→Fr D-line dashboard built by compare_elements.py. Machine-readable companion: data_json/comparison_table.json only (HTML under plots/). Physics definitions live in Section 08; this section is the lab-facing legend for every column, control, and trend. Neutral alkalis only; ions and 3d metals are unsupported here (same scope as polarizability and Feshbach).

How to build it

Prefers per-element data_json/<el>_lifetimes.json (and the Rydberg / ORCA inputs those lifetimes were built from). If a lifetimes JSON is missing, that element falls back to hardcoded literature D-line sheets inside compare_elements.py (Steck / NIST ASD) and is tagged orange in the HTML. Default run covers all six alkalis at $T=300$ K:

python compare_elements.py python compare_elements.py --T 500 --elements Li Na K Rb Cs Fr

Writes plots/comparison_table.html and data_json/comparison_table.json. Regenerate after any lifetimes / NIST refresh so the HTML, JSON, and the trend snapshot below stay in sync:

python lifetimes.py Na # repeat per element: Li K Rb Cs Fr python compare_elements.py # rebuild CompTable

The near-resonant MOT widget for one line is separate: python scattering_rate.py --element Na --line D2 (see Laser Cooling).

Layout

One row per element (Li … Fr), sorted by $Z$ by default. Each row carries both D-lines as side-by-side column groups: D₁ ($np_{1/2}$, weaker / longer $\lambda$) and D₂ ($np_{3/2}$, stronger cycling line). Natural-linewidth, Doppler, and laser-cooling blocks on the right are quoted for D₂ (the usual MOT / cycling transition).

Source colour on $\tau$ / $f$ cells (legend at the top of the HTML): NIST · pipeline · ORCA · literature (fallback when lifetimes JSON is absent) · precision (curated Fr D-line values). Row-level source in the JSON is usually pipeline even when the D-line $f$ itself is NIST.

Formulas behind the numbers

Natural width, peak cross-section, saturation intensity
$$\Delta\nu_{\rm nat}=\frac{A}{2\pi},\qquad \sigma_{\rm peak}^{\rm nat}=\frac{\lambda^2}{4}\frac{g_u}{g_l},\qquad I_{\rm sat}=\frac{h\nu}{2\sigma_{\rm peak}\,\tau}$$

The $\lambda^2/4$ form is the pipeline natural peak ($\sigma=(\lambda^2/8\pi)(g_u/g_l)(A/\Delta\nu_{\rm nat})$ with $\Delta\nu_{\rm nat}=A/(2\pi)$), matching lifetimes.py and this CompTable. It is not the unit-normalised Lorentzian peak $\lambda^2/(2\pi)\,(g_u/g_l)$ used in Steck's isotropic sheet; see Section 07 for both conventions side by side.

Doppler FWHM and peak $\sigma$ (recomputed in-browser when $T$ changes)
$$\Delta\nu_D=\nu_0\sqrt{8\ln 2\cdot\frac{k_BT}{mc^2}},\qquad \nu_0=\frac{c}{\lambda}$$ $$\sigma_D=\sigma_{\rm nat}\cdot\frac{\Delta\nu_{\rm nat}}{\Delta\nu_D}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\ln 2}{\pi}}$$
Cooling extremes (D₂)
$$R_{\rm max}=\frac{A}{2},\qquad F_{\rm max}=\hbar k\,R_{\rm max},\qquad v_{\rm rec}=\frac{\hbar k}{m},\qquad T_{\rm rec}=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2mk_B}$$

Footnotes in the HTML repeat the isotropic-light / two-level caveats. Full OBE scattering rate $R_{\rm scatt}(I,\Delta)$ is in Section 08 and scattering_rate.py.

  • Temperature slider / input (1–2000 K, typed up to 5000 K): recomputes all Doppler columns live. Drag toward 1 K to approach the natural limit; drag toward 2000 K for a hot oven source.
  • Natural and Doppler columns are shown together (no toggle). Natural values stay fixed; Doppler values follow $T$.
  • Click any column header: sort the whole table by that quantity; click again to reverse. Useful for “smallest $I_{\rm sat}$” or “largest $E_{\rm rec}$”.
  • Highlight dropdown: colour-codes rows by $\tau$, $I_{\rm sat}$ (nat or Doppler), SOC, $\delta_p$, $R_{\rm scatt}$, or $E_{\rm rec}$. Green = smallest, yellow = middle, red = largest.
  • Export CSV: downloads the currently computed values for papers / plots.

Sources and caveats

  • Preferred $f$/$A$/$\tau$: NIST ASD via the lifetimes stack; curated Fr D-line values use the precision tag; ORCA TD-DFT only when needed.
  • If <el>_lifetimes.json is missing, that row is built from hardcoded Steck / NIST sheets and coloured literature. With lifetimes present, the row source is pipeline even when the D-line $f$ itself is NIST.
  • $\sigma_{\rm peak}$ and $I_{\rm sat}$ use the pipeline isotropic / two-level convention ($\lambda^2/4$). Compare carefully to Steck cycling ($\approx6.26$ mW/cm² for Na D2) or Steck isotropic Lorentzian ($\approx9.4$ mW/cm²) - see Section 07.
  • Doppler columns ignore pressure / transit-time / laser linewidth; they are the pure thermal Gaussian piece.
  • Highlight / sort / CSV use the current display order (including live Doppler values at the selected $T$).
